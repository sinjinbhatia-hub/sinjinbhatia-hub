## A. Warm-up

**A1.** (a) $\cos(kx + \omega t) = \cos(k(x + ct))$ is a function of $x + ct$: the wave travels in $-\hat{\mathbf x}$. $f = c/\lambda = 2.998\times10^8/5.00\times10^{-7} = 5.996\times10^{14}$ Hz, $\omega = 2\pi f = 3.767\times10^{15}$ rad/s, $k = 2\pi/\lambda = 1.257\times10^7$ rad/m.

(b) With $\mathbf k = -k\hat{\mathbf x}$, $\cos(kx + \omega t) = \cos(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r - \omega t)$, so (3.6) applies with $\hat{\mathbf k} = -\hat{\mathbf x}$ (it holds for the real fields because $\hat{\mathbf k}$ is real): $\mathbf B = \frac1c(-\hat{\mathbf x})\times\hat{\mathbf y}\,E_0\cos(kx + \omega t) = -\frac{E_0}c\hat{\mathbf z}\cos(kx + \omega t)$, with $E_0/c = 300/2.998\times10^8 = 1.001\times10^{-6}$ T. Check: $\mathbf E\times\mathbf B\propto\hat{\mathbf y}\times(-\hat{\mathbf z}) = -\hat{\mathbf x}$, the direction of travel.

(c) $I = \tfrac12c\varepsilon_0E_0^2 = \tfrac12(2.998\times10^8)(8.854\times10^{-12})(9\times10^4) = 119.4$ W/m².

**A2.** $I = P/(\pi r^2) = 5.0\times10^{-3}/(\pi\times(0.5\times10^{-3})^2) = 6366$ W/m². From (3.7), $E_0 = \sqrt{2I/(c\varepsilon_0)} = 2190$ V/m and $E_{\rm rms} = E_0/\sqrt2 = 1549$ V/m. A mirror receives pressure $2I/c$ over the beam area, so the force is $2P/c = 2\times5.0\times10^{-3}/2.998\times10^8 = 3.336\times10^{-11}$ N.

**A3.** (a) Unpolarized light is an equal incoherent mix of any two orthogonal states, so the first polarizer passes half: $I_0/2$, polarized along $x$, $\mathbf J = (1, 0)$ in amplitude units with $|\mathbf J|^2 = I_0/2$. The $90°$ polarizer gives $P(90°)(1, 0)^\top = (0, 0)$: nothing. With the $45°$ polarizer in between: $P(45°)(1, 0)^\top = \cos45°\,(\cos45°, \sin45°) = \tfrac12(1, 1)$, then $P(90°)\tfrac12(1, 1)^\top = \tfrac12(0, 1)$. Intensity fraction $\tfrac14$ of $I_0/2$: **$I_0/8$**. The middle polarizer *adds* light because it is not a filter that removes a component; it projects onto a new direction that overlaps both.

(b) With $W(\pi) = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$ and (3.10), $R(\theta)\operatorname{diag}(1, -1)R(\theta)^\top$ has entries $\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta = \cos2\theta$, $2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \sin2\theta$, $\sin^2\theta - \cos^2\theta = -\cos2\theta$: the stated matrix. (The physical plate may add an overall phase $e^{i\phi}$, which does not change the polarization.) Acting on $(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$: first entry $\cos2\theta\cos\varphi + \sin2\theta\sin\varphi = \cos(2\theta - \varphi)$, second $\sin2\theta\cos\varphi - \cos2\theta\sin\varphi = \sin(2\theta - \varphi)$. So linear light at $\varphi$ leaves linear at $2\theta - \varphi$: a reflection of the polarization direction across the fast axis. At $\theta = 22.5°$, horizontal ($\varphi = 0$) becomes $45°$; the matrix is $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$, the Hadamard gate (project Part 2 asserts this).

**A4.** (a) $R = ((1.33 - 1)/(2.33))^2 = 0.02006$. (b) $\theta_B = \arctan1.33 = 53.06°$. (c) $\theta_c = \arcsin(1/1.33) = 48.75°$. (d) The critical angle. Light from the whole sky, arriving at the surface at every angle up to grazing ($90°$), refracts into the water at angles up to $\theta_c$ (Snell run backwards: $\sin\theta_{\rm water} = \sin\theta_{\rm air}/1.33\le1/1.33$). So the entire hemisphere of sky appears inside a cone of half-angle $48.75°$ around the vertical. Outside it the diver sees light totally internally reflected from below.

**A5.** $\sigma/(\varepsilon\omega) = 4/(81\times8.854\times10^{-12}\times2\pi\times10^4) = 8.88\times10^4\gg1$: a good conductor (still $888$ at 1 MHz). By (3.21), $\delta = \sqrt{2/(\mu_0\sigma\omega)}$: at 10 kHz, $\mu_0\sigma\omega = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\times4\times2\pi\times10^4 = 0.3158$, $\delta = \sqrt{2/0.3158} = 2.52$ m; at 1 MHz, $\delta = 2.52/\sqrt{100} = 0.252$ m. A field falls by $e^{-1}$ every $\delta$. To reach a submarine tens of metres down the skin depth must be metres or more, which forces $f\lesssim$ tens of kHz: very low frequency (VLF) and below, with huge antennas and tiny data rates.

## B. Core

**B1.** The left part of the string pulls on the right part with transverse force $F_y = -T\,\partial_zf$ (small slopes), so the power transmitted across a point is $F_y\,\partial_tf = -T\,\partial_zf\,\partial_tf$. For $f = A\cos(kz - \omega t)$: $\partial_zf = -kA\sin(\cdot)$, $\partial_tf = \omega A\sin(\cdot)$, so the power is $Tk\omega A^2\sin^2(\cdot)$, average $\tfrac12Tk\omega A^2$. With $k = \omega/v$ and $T/v = T/\sqrt{T/\mu} = \sqrt{T\mu}$: $\langle P\rangle = \tfrac12\sqrt{T\mu}\,\omega^2A^2$.

(a) Write $Z_j = \sqrt{T\mu_j} = Tk_j/\omega$, so $Z_2/Z_1 = k_2/k_1$. From (3.4):
$$\frac{P_R}{P_I} = \Big(\frac{k_1 - k_2}{k_1 + k_2}\Big)^2,\qquad\frac{P_T}{P_I} = \frac{Z_2}{Z_1}\Big(\frac{2k_1}{k_1 + k_2}\Big)^2 = \frac{4k_1k_2}{(k_1 + k_2)^2}.$$
Sum: $\frac{(k_1 - k_2)^2 + 4k_1k_2}{(k_1 + k_2)^2} = \frac{(k_1 + k_2)^2}{(k_1 + k_2)^2} = 1$.

(b) Since $k_j\propto Z_j$: $\tilde A_R/\tilde A_I = (Z_1 - Z_2)/(Z_1 + Z_2)$, $\tilde A_T/\tilde A_I = 2Z_1/(Z_1 + Z_2)$. For light, (3.13) with $n_j = Z_0/Z_j$ reads $r = (1/Z_1 - 1/Z_2)/(1/Z_1 + 1/Z_2)$, $t = (2/Z_1)/(1/Z_1 + 1/Z_2)$. So the string's $Z$ corresponds to light's *admittance* $1/Z = n/Z_0$. The reason: the string's continuous quantity $f$ plays the role of $\mathbf E^\parallel$, and its "force" $T\partial_zf$, which is $Z\times$ velocity, plays the role of $\mathbf H^\parallel = \mathbf E/Z$. In both systems reflection vanishes when the impedances match, and the reflected amplitude flips sign going toward the larger $Z_{\rm string}$ (larger $n$).

**B2.** Geometry as in §3.10: $\hat{\mathbf k}_I = (\sin\theta_I, 0, \cos\theta_I)$, $\hat{\mathbf k}_R = (\sin\theta_I, 0, -\cos\theta_I)$, $\hat{\mathbf k}_T = (\sin\theta_T, 0, \cos\theta_T)$, and all three $\mathbf E$'s along $\hat{\mathbf y}$ with amplitudes $\tilde E_{0I}$, $\tilde E_{0R}$, $\tilde E_{0T}$.

*Magnetic fields.* $(a, 0, c)\times(0, 1, 0) = (-c, 0, a)$. So by (3.6) with $c\to v$:
$\tilde{\mathbf B}_I = \frac{\tilde E_{0I}}{v_1}(-\cos\theta_I, 0, \sin\theta_I)$, $\tilde{\mathbf B}_R = \frac{\tilde E_{0R}}{v_1}(\cos\theta_I, 0, \sin\theta_I)$, $\tilde{\mathbf B}_T = \frac{\tilde E_{0T}}{v_2}(-\cos\theta_T, 0, \sin\theta_T)$.

*The four conditions at $z = 0$* (common phase factor dropped, by §3.9):
1. $D^\perp$: no $\mathbf E$ has a $z$ component. $0 = 0$.
2. $\mathbf E^\parallel$ ($y$): $\tilde E_{0I} + \tilde E_{0R} = \tilde E_{0T}$.
3. $B^\perp$ ($z$): $\frac{\sin\theta_I}{v_1}(\tilde E_{0I} + \tilde E_{0R}) = \frac{\sin\theta_T}{v_2}\tilde E_{0T}$. Since $\sin\theta_I/v_1 = n_1\sin\theta_I/c = n_2\sin\theta_T/c = \sin\theta_T/v_2$ by Snell, this is condition 2 again.
4. $\mathbf H^\parallel$ ($x$): $\frac1{\mu_1v_1}\cos\theta_I(-\tilde E_{0I} + \tilde E_{0R}) = -\frac1{\mu_2v_2}\cos\theta_T\tilde E_{0T}$, i.e. $\tilde E_{0I} - \tilde E_{0R} = \frac{\mu_1v_1\cos\theta_T}{\mu_2v_2\cos\theta_I}\tilde E_{0T} = \alpha\beta\,\tilde E_{0T}$, with $\alpha = \cos\theta_T/\cos\theta_I$, $\beta = \mu_1v_1/(\mu_2v_2) = \mu_1n_2/(\mu_2n_1)$.

*Solve.* Adding 2 and 4: $2\tilde E_{0I} = (1 + \alpha\beta)\tilde E_{0T}$, so $t_s = 2/(1 + \alpha\beta)$. Then $\tilde E_{0R} = \tilde E_{0T} - \tilde E_{0I}$ gives $r_s = (1 - \alpha\beta)/(1 + \alpha\beta)$. For $\mu_1 = \mu_2$, $\alpha\beta = n_2\cos\theta_T/(n_1\cos\theta_I)$; multiplying numerator and denominator by $n_1\cos\theta_I$ gives (3.17).

*Energy.* $T_s = \frac{I_T\cos\theta_T}{I_I\cos\theta_I} = \frac{n_2\cos\theta_T}{n_1\cos\theta_I}|t_s|^2 = \alpha\beta|t_s|^2$, so $R_s + T_s = \frac{(1 - \alpha\beta)^2 + 4\alpha\beta}{(1 + \alpha\beta)^2} = 1$.

*No s Brewster angle.* $r_s = 0$ requires $n_1\cos\theta_I = n_2\cos\theta_T$. Snell gives $n_1\sin\theta_I = n_2\sin\theta_T$. Square and add: $n_1^2 = n_2^2$, contradicting $n_1\ne n_2$. So s light always reflects, which is why light reflected at $\theta_B$ is pure s.

**B3.** (a) Unpolarized light is half s and half p, incoherently, so $R = (R_s + R_p)/2 = (0.09201 + 0.008466)/2 = 0.05024$: 5.0% of the power reflects.
(b) The reflected s and p powers are $R_sI_0/2$ and $R_pI_0/2$, incoherent with each other. An analyser along s passes $I_{\max} = R_sI_0/2$; along p, $I_{\min} = R_pI_0/2$. Degree of polarization $(R_s - R_p)/(R_s + R_p) = (0.09201 - 0.008466)/(0.10049) = 0.8315$.
(c) For light reflected off a horizontal surface toward your eye, the plane of incidence is vertical (it contains the vertical normal and the ray). s polarization is perpendicular to that plane: horizontal. Since $R_s > R_p$ at every angle and $R_p = 0$ at Brewster's angle, glare is predominantly horizontally polarized. A polarizer with a vertical transmission axis passes p and blocks s, removing most of the glare while passing half of the unpolarized light from everything else. The suppression is complete at the Brewster angle, measured from the vertical normal: $53.06°$ for water (A4).

**B4.** (a) In medium 2 the wave vector is $\tilde{\mathbf k}_T = (k_x, 0, i\kappa)$ with $k_x = n_1(\omega/c)\sin\theta_I$, $\kappa = (\omega/c)\sqrt{n_1^2\sin^2\theta_I - n_2^2}$ (§3.11). Faraday for a field $\propto e^{i(\tilde{\mathbf k}\cdot\mathbf r - \omega t)}$ still gives $\tilde{\mathbf B} = \tilde{\mathbf k}\times\tilde{\mathbf E}/\omega$, and $(k_x, 0, i\kappa)\times\hat{\mathbf y} = (-i\kappa, 0, k_x)$. With $\tilde{\mathbf E}_T = E_te^{i\phi}e^{-\kappa z}e^{i(k_xx - \omega t)}\hat{\mathbf y}$ ($E_t$, $\phi$ real), the real fields are
$$\mathbf E_T = E_te^{-\kappa z}\cos\psi\,\hat{\mathbf y},\qquad\mathbf B_T = \frac{E_t}\omega e^{-\kappa z}\big(\kappa\sin\psi\,\hat{\mathbf x} + k_x\cos\psi\,\hat{\mathbf z}\big),\qquad\psi = k_xx - \omega t + \phi,$$
using $\operatorname{Re}(-ie^{i\psi}) = \sin\psi$.
(b) $\hat{\mathbf y}\times(B_x, 0, B_z) = (B_z, 0, -B_x)$, so $S_x = E_yB_z/\mu_0 = \frac{k_xE_t^2}{\mu_0\omega}e^{-2\kappa z}\cos^2\psi$ and $S_z = -E_yB_x/\mu_0 = -\frac{\kappa E_t^2}{\mu_0\omega}e^{-2\kappa z}\sin\psi\cos\psi$. Averages: $\langle\cos^2\psi\rangle = \tfrac12$, $\langle\sin\psi\cos\psi\rangle = 0$. So $\langle S_x\rangle = \frac{k_xE_t^2}{2\mu_0\omega}e^{-2\kappa z}\ne0$ (energy flows along the surface) and $\langle S_z\rangle = 0$: no net energy crosses into medium 2, consistent with $|r_s| = 1$. (Energy sloshes in and out each half-cycle: $S_z$ oscillates at $2\omega$.)
(c) By (3.19), $d = 633/(2\pi\sqrt{2.25\times0.75 - 1}) = 633/(2\pi\times0.8292) = 121.5$ nm. With $a = n_1\cos\theta_I = 0.75$ and $b = n_2|\cos\theta_T| = \sqrt{0.6875} = 0.8292$: $r_s = \frac{a - ib}{a + ib} = \frac{(a - ib)^2}{a^2 + b^2} = \frac{0.5625 - 0.6875 - 1.2437i}{1.25} = -0.1000 - 0.9950i$. $|r_s| = 1.000000$, phase $-2\arctan(b/a) = -95.74°$.

**B5.** (a) In (3.23) with $\omega_0 = \gamma = 0$: $-m\omega^2\tilde x_0 = q\tilde E_0$, so $\tilde x_0 = -q\tilde E_0/(m\omega^2)$: the electron moves opposite to the force, $180°$ out of phase. $\tilde P = Nq\tilde x = -\frac{Nq^2}{m\omega^2}\tilde E = \varepsilon_0\chi_e\tilde E$ with $\chi_e = -\omega_p^2/\omega^2$, $\omega_p^2 = Nq^2/(m\varepsilon_0)$. Above $\omega_p$, $k = \sqrt{\omega^2 - \omega_p^2}/c < \omega/c$, so $v_p = c/\sqrt{1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2} > c$ and $v_g = c\sqrt{1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2} < c$.

(d) For $f\gg f_p$, $1/v_g = \frac1c(1 - f_p^2/f^2)^{-1/2}\approx\frac1c\big(1 + \frac{f_p^2}{2f^2}\big)$, so the extra delay over a path $L$ is $\frac{L}{c}\frac{f_p^2}{2f^2}\propto\frac{NL}{f^2}$ (for a varying density, $\propto\int N\,dl/f^2$). A receiver timing the same signal on two frequencies sees arrival times $t_j = t_0 + K/f_j^2$ with unknown true time $t_0$ and unknown $K$: two equations, two unknowns, $t_0 = (f_1^2t_1 - f_2^2t_2)/(f_1^2 - f_2^2)$. ## C. Challenge

**C1.** (a) Write $D = \omega_0^2 - \omega^2$ and $n - 1 = \frac F2\,\frac D{D^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2}$ from (3.25). At $\omega_0$, $D = 0$, so $n = 1$. Differentiate with the quotient rule; at $D = 0$ only the term with $dD/d\omega = -2\omega_0$ survives: $\frac{dn}{d\omega} = \frac F2\cdot\frac{(-2\omega_0)(\gamma^2\omega_0^2)}{(\gamma^2\omega_0^2)^2} = -\frac F{\gamma^2\omega_0}$. Then $n_g = n + \omega_0\,dn/d\omega = 1 - F/\gamma^2$. From (3.25), $\alpha(\omega_0) = \frac{F\omega_0^2}c\cdot\frac\gamma{\gamma^2\omega_0^2} = \frac F{c\gamma}$.
(b) $F = 5\times10^{-5}$: $n_g = 1 - 0.5 = 0.5$, $v_g = 2c$, $1/\alpha = c\gamma/F = 200$. $F = 2\times10^{-4}$: $n_g = 1 - 2 = -1$, $v_g = -c$, $1/\alpha = 50$. The project (Part 9, "[C1]") differentiates $\operatorname{Re}\tilde k$ of the exact (3.24) numerically and gets $n_g = 0.5000$ and $-0.9997$, and $1/\alpha = 200.0$ and $50.0$. The code: `kd = lambda w: w*np.sqrt(1 + F/(1 - w**2 - 0.01j*w))`, then `((kd(1+h) - kd(1-h))/(2*h)).real` and `1/(2*kd(1.0).imag)`.
(c) Negative $v_g$ means $dk/d\omega < 0$: the envelope's arrival time at depth $z$, $t = z/v_g$, decreases with $z$. A pulse peak would leave the far face of a slab of thickness $L$ a time $L/|v_g|$ *before* the input peak enters the front face. But (3.29) is valid only for a pulse whose bandwidth is much less than $\gamma$, i.e. a pulse much longer than $1/\gamma = 100$ time units; the advance $L/|v_g|$ for a slab one absorption length thick is $50$ time units, a fraction of the pulse duration, and the transmitted intensity is down by $e^{-1}$ per absorption length. The medium reshapes the pulse: it absorbs the back of the pulse more than the front, so the transmitted peak is formed from the leading edge, which had already arrived. Nothing about the front of the pulse (where any new information must sit) moves faster than $c$: a front contains arbitrarily high frequencies, where $\tilde\varepsilon\to1$ and the speed is $c$.

**C2.** (a) With $n = n_2/n_1$ and $K = \sqrt{\sin^2\theta/n^2 - 1}$ (so $\cos\theta_T = iK$): $r_s = \frac{n_1\cos\theta - in_2K}{n_1\cos\theta + in_2K}$, phase $\phi_s = -2\arctan v$ with $v = \frac{n_2K}{n_1\cos\theta}$; $-r_p = \frac{n_2\cos\theta - in_1K}{n_2\cos\theta + in_1K}$, phase $\phi_p = -2\arctan u$ with $u = \frac{n_1K}{n_2\cos\theta}$. So $\Delta = \phi_s - \phi_p = 2(\arctan u - \arctan v)$ and $\tan\frac\Delta2 = \frac{u - v}{1 + uv}$. Now $u - v = \frac{K(n_1^2 - n_2^2)}{n_1n_2\cos\theta}$ and $1 + uv = 1 + \frac{K^2}{\cos^2\theta} = \frac{\cos^2\theta + K^2}{\cos^2\theta}$, with $\cos^2\theta + K^2 = \frac{\sin^2\theta}{n^2} - \sin^2\theta = \sin^2\theta\frac{n_1^2 - n_2^2}{n_2^2}$. Dividing: $\tan\frac\Delta2 = \frac{Kn_2\cos\theta}{n_1\sin^2\theta} = \frac{\cos\theta\sqrt{\sin^2\theta - n^2}}{\sin^2\theta}$, using $Kn_2/n_1 = Kn = \sqrt{\sin^2\theta - n^2}$.
(b) $n = 1/1.51$, $n^2 = 0.43858$, $\theta_c = \arcsin n = 41.47°$. Put $s = \sin^2\theta$; then $\tan^2(\Delta/2) = (1 - s)(s - n^2)/s^2$. *The $45°$ angles.* With $\tau = \tan^2 22.5° = (\sqrt2 - 1)^2 = 0.17157$, the condition $(1 - s)(s - n^2) = \tau s^2$ is the quadratic $(1 + \tau)s^2 - (1 + n^2)s + n^2 = 0$: $s = \big[1.43858\pm\sqrt{1.43858^2 - 4(1.17157)(0.43858)}\big]/(2\times1.17157) = (1.43858\pm0.11914)/2.34315 = 0.56312$ or $0.66480$, i.e. $\theta = 48.62°$ or $54.62°$. Check $48.62°$ directly: $\cos\theta = 0.6611$, $\tan(\Delta/2) = 0.6611\times\sqrt{0.56312 - 0.43858}/0.56312 = 0.4142$, $\Delta/2 = 22.50°$. *The maximum.* $\tan^2(\Delta/2) = -1 + (1 + n^2)/s - n^2/s^2$; its $s$-derivative vanishes at $s = 2n^2/(1 + n^2) = 0.60968$ ($\theta = 51.34°$), where it equals $(1 - n^2)^2/(4n^2)$. So $\tan(\Delta_{\max}/2) = (1 - n^2)/(2n) = 0.42387$, $\Delta_{\max} = 45.94°$: the retardance only just clears $45°$, which is why the rhomb angle is pinned to one of two values. The project (Part 9, "[C2]") confirms both by scanning $\theta$ from $\theta_c$ to grazing.
(c) Send light linearly polarized at $45°$ to the plane of incidence into a glass rhomb cut so that it reflects twice at $54.6°$. Equal s and p amplitudes; each reflection keeps $|r| = 1$ and adds $45°$ of relative phase, so after two the components are equal in size and $90°$ apart: circular light. The retardance depends only on $n$ and $\theta$, not on $\lambda$ directly, and $n$ of glass changes little across the visible (the project's toy glass goes from 1.4687 at 400 nm to 1.4539 at 700 nm). At $54.62°$, $\Delta$ per reflection is $44.47°$ for $n_1 = 1.50$ and $45.51°$ for $n_1 = 1.52$: a change of about 2% for an index change larger than the whole visible dispersion. A birefringent plate's retardance $\Gamma = (n_s - n_f)\omega d/c$ is proportional to $1/\lambda$, so a plate that is quarter-wave at 550 nm has $550/450$ times too much retardance at 450 nm.

**C3.** (a) $\psi(x, 0) = \int\phi(k)e^{ikx}dk$ with $\phi(k)\propto e^{-\sigma_0^2(k - k_0)^2}$: this is step 1 of §3.14 with $t\to-x$ (the Gaussian pair), and $|\psi(x, 0)|^2\propto e^{-x^2/(2\sigma_0^2)}$. Let $q = k - k_0$. Exactly, $\omega(k_0 + q) = \omega_0 + \frac{\hbar k_0}mq + \frac\hbar{2m}q^2$. Then
$$\psi(x, t)\propto e^{i(k_0x - \omega_0t)}\int\exp\Big[-\Big(\sigma_0^2 + \frac{i\hbar t}{2m}\Big)q^2 + iq\Big(x - \frac{\hbar k_0}mt\Big)\Big]dq = e^{i(k_0x - \omega_0t)}\sqrt{\frac\pi a}\,e^{-(x - \hbar k_0t/m)^2/(4a)},$$
with $a = \sigma_0^2 + i\hbar t/2m$ (step 3 of §3.14; $\operatorname{Re}a > 0$). As in step 4, $|\psi|^2$ is Gaussian with standard deviation $|a|/\sigma_0 = \sigma_0\sqrt{1 + (\hbar t/2m\sigma_0^2)^2}$, centred at $x = \hbar k_0t/m$: the group velocity $d\omega/dk$ at $k_0$, which is the classical velocity $p/m$.
(b) Doubling: $(\hbar t/2m\sigma_0^2)^2 = 3$, $t_2 = 2\sqrt3\,m\sigma_0^2/\hbar$. Electron, $\sigma_0 = 10^{-10}$ m: $t_2 = 2\sqrt3\times9.109\times10^{-31}\times10^{-20}/1.055\times10^{-34} = 2.992\times10^{-16}$ s. Marble, $m = 10^{-3}$ kg, $\sigma_0 = 10^{-6}$ m: $t_2 = 3.285\times10^{19}$ s $= 1.04\times10^{12}$ years, far longer than the age of the universe. Quantum spreading is invisible for everyday masses because $\hbar/m$ is tiny.
(c) $\omega''(k) = \hbar/m$ plays the role of $k''(\omega)$, with $t$ in place of $z$: the spreading parameter $b = k''z$ becomes $\omega''t$. For a free particle $\omega(k)$ is exactly quadratic, so the second-order expansion is the whole story and (3.30) is exact. For light in glass, $k(\omega)$ from (3.24) has nonzero third and higher derivatives, so (3.30) is the leading approximation; the project's FFT propagation, which uses the full $k(\omega)$, differs from it by 2% at the deepest probe.
