## A. Warm-up

**A1.** The cross-section is $A = \pi(10^{-3})^2 = 3.1416\times10^{-6}$ m².
(a) $R = L/\sigma A = 10/(3.77\times10^7\times3.1416\times10^{-6}) = 0.08443\ \Omega$; $P = I^2R = 25(0.08443) = 2.111$ W.
(b) $E = J/\sigma = I/\sigma A = 4.222\times10^{-2}$ V/m (check: $IR/L = 0.4222/10$).
(c) $v_d = I/(neA) = 5/(1.81\times10^{29}\times1.602\times10^{-19}\times3.1416\times10^{-6}) = 5.488\times10^{-5}$ m/s.
(d) $\tau = m\sigma/ne^2 = (9.109\times10^{-31})(3.77\times10^7)/(1.81\times10^{29}\times(1.602\times10^{-19})^2) = 7.39\times10^{-15}$ s, about a third of copper's $2.5\times10^{-14}$ s: aluminium has twice the electron density of copper and lower conductivity, so its electrons collide more often.

**A2.** (a) $\Phi = B_0A\sin\omega t$ with $A = 0.02$ m², so $\mathcal E = -d\Phi/dt = -B_0A\omega\cos\omega t$. Peak: $B_0A\omega = (0.05)(0.02)(2\pi\cdot60) = 0.3770$ V; peak current $0.3770/0.5 = 0.7540$ A.
(b) At $t = 0$, $B = 0$ but $dB/dt = B_0\omega > 0$ toward you. Orient the loop counterclockwise as you see it, so $d\mathbf a$ points toward you and $\Phi$ is increasing: $\mathcal E = -d\Phi/dt < 0$, so current flows against the chosen orientation, **clockwise**. Lenz agrees: the induced current must make field pointing away from you inside the loop, and a clockwise current does.
(c) Orient the ring counterclockwise, with $P$ at the bottom and $Q$ at the top. The current is $I = \mathcal E/R_r$ counterclockwise, and inside the resistive ring $\mathbf J = \sigma\mathbf E$, so along each half (in the current's direction) $\int\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l = IR_r/2 = \mathcal E/2$. An ideal voltmeter reads $\int\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l$ through itself, from $P$ to $Q$ say.
*Leads on the left.* The closed path "$P\to Q$ through the meter (outside left), then $Q\to P$ along the left half of the ring (counterclockwise direction)" encloses no flux (the solenoid is inside the ring, not between the leads and the left half). So $\int_{\rm meter}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l + \mathcal E/2 = 0$: the reading is $-\mathcal E/2$.
*Leads on the right.* The path "$P\to Q$ along the right half (counterclockwise), then $Q\to P$ through the meter" encloses no flux: $\mathcal E/2 + \int_{Q\to P}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l = 0$, so $\int_{P\to Q} = +\mathcal E/2$.
Same two points, opposite readings. The two measurement loops differ by a loop that encircles the changing flux, and $\nabla\times\mathbf E\ne0$ there, so there is no single "potential difference between $P$ and $Q$". Lumped circuit theory (Kirchhoff's voltage law) assumes every changing flux is confined inside components, so that no measurement loop encircles one.

**A3.** $n = 400/0.2 = 2000$/m. (a) $L = \mu_0N^2\pi R^2/\ell = 4\pi\times10^{-7}(1.6\times10^5)\pi(2.25\times10^{-4})/0.2 = 7.106\times10^{-4}$ H; $W = \tfrac12(7.106\times10^{-4})(9) = 3.198\times10^{-3}$ J.
(b) $B = \mu_0nI = 7.540$ mT, $u_B = B^2/2\mu_0 = 22.62$ J/m³. Volume $\pi(0.015)^2(0.2) = 1.4137\times10^{-4}$ m³; $u_BV = 3.198\times10^{-3}$ J $= W$.
(c) $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2 = B^2/2\mu_0$ gives $E = cB = 2.26\times10^6$ V/m, three quarters of air's breakdown field, to match a modest 7.5 mT coil.

**A4.** $C = \varepsilon_0\pi a^2/d = 8.854\times10^{-12}(2.827\times10^{-3})/(2\times10^{-3}) = 1.2517\times10^{-11}$ F. Between the plates $E = V/d$, so the displacement current is $\varepsilon_0A\,\partial_tE = C\,dV/dt$, with peak $CV_0\omega = (1.2517\times10^{-11})(50)(2\pi\times10^3) = 3.932\times10^{-6}$ A, peak density $I_d/\pi a^2 = 1.391\times10^{-3}$ A/m², and by (3.19) on the rim circle $B = \mu_0I_d/2\pi a = 2.62\times10^{-11}$ T: two million times weaker than the Earth's field. Ampère worked with steady currents, and even at kHz the term is unmeasurably small in the lab. It matters when $\varepsilon_0\omega E$ is comparable to the conduction current density, i.e. at high frequency or in empty space.

**A5.** (a) $\mathbf E = -\nabla V = E_0\hat{\mathbf x}$, $\mathbf B = 0$.
(b) $V' = V - \partial_t\lambda = -E_0x - (-E_0x) = 0$, $\mathbf A' = \nabla\lambda = -E_0t\,\hat{\mathbf x}$. Then $\mathbf E' = -\nabla V' - \partial_t\mathbf A' = E_0\hat{\mathbf x}$ and $\mathbf B' = \nabla\times(-E_0t\hat{\mathbf x}) = 0$. Unchanged.
(c) Pair 1: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A = 0$ (Coulomb) and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A + c^{-2}\partial_tV = 0 + 0$ (Lorenz). Pair 2: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A' = \partial_x(-E_0t) = 0$ and $\partial_tV' = 0$, so both conditions again. Both pairs satisfy both gauges because $\lambda = -E_0xt$ has $\nabla^2\lambda = 0$ and $\Box\lambda = 0$: it lies in the residual freedom of each gauge. A gauge condition does not make the potentials unique. (Pair 2 is also in the "temporal gauge" $V = 0$.)

**A6.** (a) For $\mathbf E = E_0\hat{\mathbf x}$: $E_iE_j = E_0^2\delta_{ix}\delta_{jx}$, so $T = \varepsilon_0E_0^2[\mathrm{diag}(1,0,0) - \tfrac12I] = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E_0^2\,\mathrm{diag}(1, -1, -1)$. Eigenvector $\hat{\mathbf x}$ (along the field) has $+\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E_0^2$: since $T_{ij}\,da_j$ is the force on the contents, a face whose outward normal is $+\hat{\mathbf x}$ is pulled outward along the lines: **tension**. $\hat{\mathbf y}$, $\hat{\mathbf z}$ have $-\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E_0^2$: faces are pushed in, **pressure**. For $\mathbf B = B_0\hat{\mathbf z}$: $T = \frac{B_0^2}{2\mu_0}\mathrm{diag}(-1, -1, 1)$, tension along $\hat{\mathbf z}$, pressure across. Values: $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0(10^6)^2 = 4.427$ Pa; $B_0^2/2\mu_0 = 3.979\times10^5$ Pa.
(b) $E_iE_j = \tfrac12E_0^2\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\1&1&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}$; subtracting $\tfrac12E_0^2I$,
$$T = \frac{\varepsilon_0E_0^2}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}.$$
The upper block $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $\pm1$ with eigenvectors $(1, 1)/\sqrt2$ and $(1, -1)/\sqrt2$. So the tension axis is $(1, 1, 0)/\sqrt2$, along $\mathbf E$, and the pressure axes are perpendicular. The tensor rotated with the field ($T' = RTR^\top$, LA-12 §3.17); in the original axes the off-diagonal $T_{xy}$ is a shear, an $x$-force on a face with normal $\hat{\mathbf y}$. The project prints eigenvalues $(-4.427, -4.427, +4.427)$ Pa and top eigenvector $(0.7071, 0.7071, 0)$.
(c) $\delta_{ii} = 3$, so $T_{ii} = \varepsilon_0(E^2 - \tfrac32E^2) + \mu_0^{-1}(B^2 - \tfrac32B^2) = -\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2 - B^2/2\mu_0 = -u$.

## B. Core

**B1.** (a) $\omega = 3000\times2\pi/60 = 314.16$ rad/s. The material velocity is $\mathbf v = \omega s\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$, so $\mathbf v\times\mathbf B = \omega sB(\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}\times\hat{\mathbf z}) = \omega Bs\,\hat{\mathbf s}$ and
$$\mathcal E = \int_0^a\omega Bs\,ds = \tfrac12\omega Ba^2 = \tfrac12(314.16)(0.5)(0.01) = 0.7854\ \text{V}.$$
Positive charges are pushed outward, so the rim is the positive terminal. Rim speed $\omega a = 31.42$ m/s.
(b) Theorem 3.1 assumes a thin wire whose material moves with the curve. Here the current flows through whichever radius is between the brushes at the moment: the circuit is fixed in space while the copper slides through it, and a fixed circuit encloses constant flux, so a naive flux rule predicts zero. Rescue it by following the material: a radius moving with the disk sweeps a sector of area $\tfrac12a^2\theta$, so it sweeps flux at rate $\tfrac12Ba^2\omega$, which is $\mathcal E$. That is the "flux cutting" form, the content of step 3 of Theorem 3.1 applied to the moving radius.
(c) $\omega Bs\,\hat{\mathbf s} = \nabla(\tfrac12\omega Bs^2)$, a gradient in the plane of the disk, so its line integral depends only on the endpoints ($s = 0$ and $s = a$). The project (Your turn 2) integrates along a radius and along a $1.5$-turn spiral and gets $0.78540$ V both times.
(d) An element $I\,ds\,\hat{\mathbf s}$ in $B\hat{\mathbf z}$ feels $d\mathbf F = I\,ds\,B(\hat{\mathbf s}\times\hat{\mathbf z}) = -IB\,ds\,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$, with torque about the axis $s\hat{\mathbf s}\times d\mathbf F = -IBs\,ds\,\hat{\mathbf z}$. Integrating, $\mathbf N = -\tfrac12IBa^2\hat{\mathbf z}$: magnitude $\tfrac12(10)(0.5)(0.01) = 0.0250$ N m, opposing $\boldsymbol\omega$ (Lenz). Keeping the disk turning takes power $|N|\omega = 0.0250\times314.16 = 7.854$ W $= \mathcal EI = 0.7854\times10$. The magnetic force again relays mechanical work into electrical work without doing any itself.

**B2.** (a) Let $y$ be the length of loop still in the field; the flux is $\Phi = B\ell y$ and $dy/dt = -v$, so $|\mathcal E| = B\ell v$ and $I = B\ell v/R$. The forces on the two vertical sides cancel (opposite currents, same field). On the top edge the force has magnitude $I\ell B = (B\ell)^2v/R$ and, by Lenz, opposes the loss of flux, i.e. points up. So $m\dot v = mg - (B\ell)^2v/R$, and at $\dot v = 0$:
$$v_t = \frac{mgR}{(B\ell)^2} = \frac{(0.005)(9.80665)(0.01)}{(1)(0.05)^2} = 0.19613\ \text{m/s}.$$
(b) $\dot v = g(1 - v/v_t)$ with $v(0) = 0$ gives $v = v_t(1 - e^{-gt/v_t})$. The time constant is $v_t/g = mR/(B\ell)^2 = 20.00$ ms, and $v(50\text{ ms}) = 0.19613(1 - e^{-2.5}) = 0.18003$ m/s (the project's ODE solver agrees).
(c) $I_t = B\ell v_t/R = (0.05)(0.19613)/0.01 = 0.9807$ A. $mgv_t = (0.005)(9.80665)(0.19613) = 9.617\times10^{-3}$ W and $I_t^2R = (0.9807)^2(0.01) = 9.617\times10^{-3}$ W. Gravitational energy becomes Joule heat in the loop.

**B3.** $\mu_0n\,dI/dt = (4\pi\times10^{-7})(1000)(500) = 0.6283$ T/s.
(a) By (3.11): at $s = 1$ cm, $|E| = \tfrac12(0.6283)(0.01) = 3.142\times10^{-3}$ V/m; at $s = 5$ cm $> R$, $|E| = \tfrac12(0.6283)(0.03)^2/0.05 = 5.655\times10^{-3}$ V/m. $E_\phi$ has the sign of $-dI/dt$: the field circulates opposite to the solenoid current (it would drive a current that opposes the growth).
(b) $\mathbf F = -e\mathbf E$ points *along* the solenoid current. $a = eE/m_e = (1.602\times10^{-19})(3.142\times10^{-3})/(9.109\times10^{-31}) = 5.525\times10^8$ m/s².
(c) The ring links all the solenoid's flux, $\Phi = \mu_0nI\pi R^2$: $|\mathcal E| = 0.6283\times\pi(0.03)^2 = 1.7765\times10^{-3}$ V and $I = 8.883$ mA. Moving the ring off-centre or enlarging it changes $\mathbf E$ along the ring point by point, but not the flux it encloses, so the circulation and the current are unchanged (ignoring the ring's self-inductance).
(d) EM-5 (3.26) gives $A_\phi = \mu_0nIs/2$ inside and $\mu_0nIR^2/2s$ outside; $-\partial_tA_\phi$ is (3.11) term by term. With no charges and the Coulomb gauge, $V = 0$ and (3.32) says $\mathbf E = -\partial_t\mathbf A$. Outside, $\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A = 0$ but $\mathbf A\ne0$ (EM-5's Aharonov–Bohm geometry), and it changes as $I$ changes, so $\mathbf E\ne0$. This is not action at a distance: the change spreads out at $c$, and the quasistatic (3.11) holds once the light-crossing time is short compared with the ramp.

**B4.** (a) Put the wire on the $z$-axis and the loop in the $xz$-plane at $s < x < s + b$, with sides of length $a$ along $z$. $B = \mu_0I/2\pi x$ is perpendicular to the loop, so
$$\Phi = \int_s^{s+b}\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi x}a\,dx = \frac{\mu_0Ia}{2\pi}\ln\frac{s + b}{s},\qquad M = (2\times10^{-7})(0.2)\ln3 = 4.394\times10^{-8}\ \text{H}.$$
Peak EMF: $M\omega I_0 = (4.394\times10^{-8})(376.99)(10) = 1.657\times10^{-4}$ V $= 0.1657$ mV.
(b) A wire must close. Close it by a parallel return wire a distance $D$ away on the far side. The return's own contribution to the flux through the loop is $\frac{\mu_0a}{2\pi}\ln\frac{D - s}{D - s - b}\to0$ as $D\to\infty$, so the circuit's $M$ tends to the value in (a). Reciprocity then says: a changing current $I_2$ in the loop induces $-M\,dI_2/dt$ around the wire's circuit, i.e. the loop's flux through the huge half-plane between the two wires is $MI_2$. Computing that flux directly would mean integrating a near-dipole field over a half-plane; (3.12) makes it unnecessary.
(c) *Stage 1:* raise $I_1$ from $0$ with $I_2 = 0$. Against loop 1's back-EMF the work is $\tfrac12L_1I_1^2$. Loop 2 has an induced EMF but no current, so no work is done there. *Stage 2:* hold $I_1$, raise $I_2$. Loop 2 costs $\tfrac12L_2I_2^2$. In loop 1 the changing $I_2$ induces $-M_{12}\,dI_2/dt$; to hold $I_1$ fixed its source supplies power $I_1M_{12}\,dI_2/dt$, total $M_{12}I_1I_2$. So $W = \tfrac12L_1I_1^2 + M_{12}I_1I_2 + \tfrac12L_2I_2^2$. The other order gives $M_{21}$ in the middle. The final state is the same, and $W = \int B^2/2\mu_0$ depends only on the final field, so $M_{12} = M_{21}$: an energy proof of reciprocity.
(d) $W = \tfrac12\mathbf I^\top\begin{pmatrix}L_1&M\\M&L_2\end{pmatrix}\mathbf I = \frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int B^2\,d\tau\ge0$ for every $\mathbf I = (I_1, I_2)$. So the symmetric inductance matrix is positive semidefinite (LA-8), which for a $2\times2$ matrix means nonnegative diagonal and nonnegative determinant: $L_1L_2 - M^2\ge0$. The coupling coefficient $k = M/\sqrt{L_1L_2}$ lies in $[-1, 1]$; a good transformer has $k$ close to 1.

**B5.** (a) Ampère: $B = \mu_0I/2\pi s$ for $a < s < b$ and $0$ outside (the outer conductor's return current cancels the inner one). Through a rectangle of length $\ell$ between the conductors, $\Phi = \frac{\mu_0I\ell}{2\pi}\ln\frac ba$, so $L/\ell = \frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\ln\frac ba = (2\times10^{-7})\ln4 = 2.773\times10^{-7}$ H/m.
(b) $\frac{W}{\ell} = \int_a^b\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\left(\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi s}\right)^22\pi s\,ds = \frac{\mu_0I^2}{4\pi}\ln\frac ba = \frac12\frac{L}{\ell}I^2$, the same $L/\ell$ (the project's quadrature gives $2.7726\times10^{-7}$ H/m).
(c) With the inner conductor at $+V$ carrying $I$ along $+\hat{\mathbf z}$ toward the load, $\mathbf B = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi s}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$ and $\hat{\mathbf s}\times\hat{\boldsymbol\phi} = \hat{\mathbf z}$:
$$\mathbf S = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\frac{V}{s\ln(b/a)}\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi s}\hat{\mathbf z} = \frac{VI}{2\pi s^2\ln(b/a)}\hat{\mathbf z},\qquad\int_a^b S\,2\pi s\,ds = VI = 200\ \text{W}.$$
$S(a) = 200/(2\pi(0.5\times10^{-3})^2\ln4) = 9.185\times10^7$ W/m² and $S(b) = 5.740\times10^6$ W/m² (ratio $(b/a)^2 = 16$). The power travels down the cable in the insulator; the copper only guides it.
(d) Resistive conductors carry a small axial $E_z$ along their current, and tangential $\mathbf E$ is continuous at their surfaces. At the inner conductor $E_z > 0$, $B_\phi > 0$, so $S_s = -E_zB_\phi/\mu_0 < 0$: into the inner conductor. At the outer conductor the current runs along $-\hat{\mathbf z}$, so $E_z < 0$ while $B_\phi > 0$ just inside it: $S_s > 0$, outward, into the outer conductor. The flow lines tilt toward both conductors and deliver their Joule heat, as in Example 4.5.

**B6.** (a) $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B) = 0 = \mu_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf J + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)$. With Gauss's law $\varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = \rho$ this is $\mu_0(\nabla\cdot\mathbf J + \partial_t\rho) = 0$, continuity; without Gauss's law the divergence of (3.18) says nothing about $\rho$. Faraday: $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf E) = 0 = -\partial_t(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B)$, so $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B$ never changes; if it is zero once, it is zero forever. The divergence equations are constraints on initial data that the curl equations preserve.
(b) Gauss for $\mathbf D$ on a pillbox around a plate gives $D = Q/A$, so $\partial_tD = I/A$. With $\mathbf D = \varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0\mathbf E$: $\varepsilon_0\partial_tE = I/(\varepsilon_rA)$ and $\partial_tP = \partial_t(D - \varepsilon_0E) = \frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r}\frac IA$. The fraction carried by moving bound charge is $(\varepsilon_r - 1)/\varepsilon_r$ (75% for $\varepsilon_r = 4$); only $1/\varepsilon_r$ is the vacuum term.
(c) *Above* ($z > 0$): $E_x = E_0\cos(\omega(t - z/c))$. $(\nabla\times\mathbf E)_y = \partial_zE_x = E_0\frac{\omega}{c}\sin(\omega(t - z/c))$, so Faraday gives $\partial_tB_y = -E_0\frac\omega c\sin(\cdot)$ and $B_y = \frac{E_0}{c}\cos(\omega(t - z/c))$. Maxwell–Ampère: $(\nabla\times\mathbf B)_x = -\partial_zB_y = -\frac{E_0\omega}{c^2}\sin(\cdot) = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_tE_x$. Both divergences vanish (fields depend on $z$ only and have no $z$-component). $\mathbf E\times\mathbf B\parallel\hat{\mathbf x}\times\hat{\mathbf y} = +\hat{\mathbf z}$: moving away from the sheet. *Below:* $E_x = E_0\cos(\omega(t + z/c))$; now $\partial_zE_x = -E_0\frac\omega c\sin(\cdot)$, which gives $B_y = -\frac{E_0}{c}\cos(\omega(t + z/c))$, travelling toward $-\hat{\mathbf z}$; Maxwell–Ampère checks the same way. At $z = 0$, with $\hat{\mathbf n} = \hat{\mathbf z}$: $E_x$ is continuous automatically. In vacuum $\mathbf H = \mathbf B/\mu_0$, so (3.23) reads $\mathbf B_{\rm above} - \mathbf B_{\rm below} = \mu_0\mathbf K\times\hat{\mathbf n}$:
$$\frac{2E_0}{c}\cos\omega t\,\hat{\mathbf y} = \mu_0K_0\cos\omega t\,(\hat{\mathbf x}\times\hat{\mathbf z}) = -\mu_0K_0\cos\omega t\,\hat{\mathbf y}\ \Rightarrow\ E_0 = -\frac{\mu_0cK_0}{2} = -\frac{Z_0K_0}{2}.$$
For $K_0 = 1$ A/m, $|E_0| = 188.37$ V/m, and at the sheet $\mathbf E$ opposes the current.
(d) The field does work $\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf K = -\frac{Z_0K_0^2}{2}\cos^2\omega t$ per area on the sheet's charges, so the source supplies $\frac{Z_0K_0^2}{2}\cos^2\omega t$, averaging $Z_0K_0^2/4 = 94.18$ W/m². Each side carries $|\mathbf S| = E_x^2/\mu_0c = E_0^2\cos^2(\cdot)/Z_0$, averaging $E_0^2/2Z_0 = Z_0K_0^2/8$; both sides together carry $Z_0K_0^2/4 = 94.18$ W/m². Poynting's theorem balances across the sheet: the source's work is radiated.

**B7.** (a) Put $+Q$ on the top plate, so between the plates $\mathbf E = -E\hat{\mathbf z}$ with $E = Q/\varepsilon_0A$, and $\mathbf E = 0$ outside. Enclose the top plate in a box; only its bottom face, in the gap, sees a field, and its outward normal is $-\hat{\mathbf z}$, so $d\mathbf a = -A\hat{\mathbf z}$ and $F_i = -T_{iz}A$. With $\mathbf E\parallel\hat{\mathbf z}$: $T_{zz} = \varepsilon_0(E^2 - \tfrac12E^2) = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$, $T_{xz} = T_{yz} = 0$. So $F_z = -\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2A = -Q^2/2\varepsilon_0A$: the plates attract, pulled by the tension of the lines between them. This is EM-2 (3.17): pressure $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$ directed from the conductor into the field region (down, for the top plate). Numbers: $E = 10^{-9}/(8.854\times10^{-12}\times10^{-4}) = 1.129\times10^6$ V/m and $F = 5.647\times10^{-4}$ N.
(b) Enclose a patch (area $A$) of winding in a pillbox. Only the inner face sees a field, $\mathbf B = B\hat{\mathbf z}$, with outward normal $-\hat{\mathbf s}$. For a field along $z$, $T_{ss} = -B^2/2\mu_0$ and $T_{zs} = 0$, so $F_s = T_{ss}(-A) = +\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}A$: **outward**, $22.62$ Pa for A3's coil, equal to $u_B$. Virtual work at fixed flux $\Phi$: $W = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}\pi R^2\ell$ with $B = \Phi/\pi R^2$ is $W = \Phi^2\ell/(2\mu_0\pi R^2)$, so $F = -\partial W/\partial R = \Phi^2\ell/(\mu_0\pi R^3) = B^2\pi R\ell/\mu_0$, and dividing by the area $2\pi R\ell$ gives $B^2/2\mu_0$ outward. The lines push sideways on each other and on the windings.

**B8.** (a)–(b) Differentiate $\sin(\omega\Delta t/2) = S\sin(k\Delta x/2)$: $\frac{\Delta t}{2}\cos\frac{\omega\Delta t}{2}\,d\omega = S\frac{\Delta x}{2}\cos\frac{k\Delta x}{2}\,dk$. With $S\Delta x/\Delta t = c$ and $\cos\frac{\omega\Delta t}{2} = \sqrt{1 - S^2\sin^2\frac{k\Delta x}{2}}$,
$$v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac{c\cos(k\Delta x/2)}{\sqrt{1 - S^2\sin^2(k\Delta x/2)}}.$$
The reference implementation (Part 10) launches the modulated pulse with $B(x, -\Delta t/2) = E(x + c\Delta t/2)/c$, records the centroid of $E^2$ after $0.1$ m and after $0.7$ m of nominal travel, and divides:

| $S$ | measured $v/c$ | $v_g/c$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0.25 | 0.97070 | 0.97077 |
| 0.5 | 0.97635 | 0.97641 |
| 0.9 | 0.99385 | 0.99386 |
| 1.0 | 1.00000 | 1.00000 |

Smaller time steps are *less* accurate here: the time error and the space error have opposite signs and cancel exactly at $S = 1$, where the relation becomes $\omega = ck$ and each step shifts the wave by exactly one cell. That is special to 1D: in 2D and 3D stability requires $S\le1/\sqrt2$ or $1/\sqrt3$, the cancellation is never complete, and the error depends on direction. At $k_0\Delta x = 0.5$ (12.6 cells per wavelength) the error is 2.4% at $S = 0.5$; practical codes use 20 or more cells per wavelength.
(c) At $k\Delta x = \pi$, $\sin(\omega\Delta t/2) = S = 1.02$. Write $\omega\Delta t/2 = \pi/2 + i\eta$: $\sin(\pi/2 + i\eta) = \cosh\eta = 1.02$, so $\eta = \operatorname{arccosh}1.02 = 0.1997$. The time factor per step is $e^{-i\omega\Delta t} = e^{-i\pi}e^{2\eta}$, of magnitude $e^{2\eta} = 1.4908$ (the conjugate root decays). Measured between steps 400 and 600: $1.4894$. Over 600 steps $1.49^{600}\approx10^{104}$, which takes round-off of $10^{-16}$ to about $10^{88}$: the $6\times10^{87}$ of Part 8. The growing mode flips sign every cell and every step, the signature of a Courant violation.

## C. Challenge

**C1.** (a) By symmetry $\mathbf E = E_\phi\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$ on the orbit and $2\pi RE_\phi = -d\Phi/dt$, so the tangential force on the electron has magnitude $e|E_\phi| = \frac{e}{2\pi R}\frac{d\Phi}{dt}$ (with the field's sign chosen to accelerate). $d\mathbf p/dt = \mathbf F$ holds relativistically with $\mathbf p = \gamma m\mathbf v$, so $dp/dt = \frac{e}{2\pi R}\frac{d\Phi}{dt}$. For a circle of radius $R$ at speed $v$ the momentum turns at rate $v/R$, so $pv/R = evB(R)$, i.e. $p = eRB(R)$, again valid relativistically. Then $eR\frac{dB(R)}{dt} = \frac{e}{2\pi R}\frac{d\Phi}{dt}$, so $\Phi = 2\pi R^2B(R)$ (both start at zero) and $\langle B\rangle = \Phi/\pi R^2 = 2B(R)$. Neither step used $p = mv$, so the condition is exact relativistically; that is why the betatron (Kerst, 1940) could take electrons to relativistic energies.
(b) $pc = eRBc = (0.5)(0.4)(2.998\times10^8)$ eV $= 59.96$ MeV. $E = \sqrt{(pc)^2 + (m_ec^2)^2} = \sqrt{59.96^2 + 0.511^2}$ MeV, so the kinetic energy is $E - m_ec^2 = 59.45$ MeV. $\Phi = \pi R^2\langle B\rangle = \pi(0.25)(0.8) = 0.6283$ Wb.
(c) EMF per turn $= \Phi/t = 0.6283/0.004 = 157.08$ V, i.e. $157.08$ eV per turn, so $59.45\times10^6/157.08 = 3.785\times10^5$ turns. Path length: $3.785\times10^5\times2\pi(0.5) = 1.189\times10^6$ m, against $c\times4$ ms $= 1.199\times10^6$ m. The electrons reach $0.9c$ at $0.661$ MeV, after $70\ \mu$s ($1.76\%$ of the ramp), so they travel at nearly $c$ almost the whole time; the small shortfall is the slow start.

**C2.** (a) Centre Maxwell–Ampère at $t_{n+1/2}$: $\varepsilon\frac{E^{n+1} - E^n}{\Delta t} = -\frac{1}{\mu_0}\frac{B_{i+1/2} - B_{i-1/2}}{\Delta x} - \sigma\frac{E^{n+1} + E^n}{2}$. Collect $E^{n+1}$: $E^{n+1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\Delta t} + \frac\sigma2\right) = E^n\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\Delta t} - \frac\sigma2\right) - \frac{1}{\mu_0\Delta x}(B_{i+1/2} - B_{i-1/2})$. Divide by $\varepsilon/\Delta t + \sigma/2$:
$$c_a = \frac{1 - \sigma\Delta t/2\varepsilon}{1 + \sigma\Delta t/2\varepsilon},\qquad c_b = \frac{\Delta t/(\varepsilon\mu_0\Delta x)}{1 + \sigma\Delta t/2\varepsilon}.$$
The average keeps second-order accuracy, and $|c_a|\le1$ for any $\sigma$, so the loss term can never destabilize the scheme.
(b) Insert $e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$: $-\varepsilon_0\omega^2 - i\sigma\omega = -k^2/\mu_0$, so $k = \frac\omega c\sqrt{1 + \frac{i\sigma}{\varepsilon_0\omega}}\approx\frac\omega c\left(1 + \frac{i\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0\omega}\right)$ for $\sigma\ll\varepsilon_0\omega$. The amplitude falls as $e^{-x\,{\rm Im}\,k}$ with ${\rm Im}\,k = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0c} = \frac{\sigma\mu_0c}{2} = \frac{\sigma Z_0}{2}$, independent of $\omega$. A pulse whose whole spectrum is in this regime decays without changing shape.
(c) The project sets $\sigma = 2/Z_0 = 5.309\times10^{-3}$ S/m so that $\alpha = 1$ m⁻¹. The peak is $0.6038$ at $x = 1.099$ m and $0.1506$ at $x = 2.499$ m: $\alpha = \ln(0.6038/0.1506)/1.400 = 0.9917$ m⁻¹, $0.8\%$ low. A Gaussian has spectral weight down to $\omega = 0$, where $\sigma/\varepsilon_0\omega$ is not small, and the telegraph equation's solution is the decaying front plus a slowly decaying wake behind it, which slightly feeds the peak. (For the pulse's typical $\omega\approx c/w = 1.5\times10^{10}$ s⁻¹, $\sigma/\varepsilon_0\omega\approx0.04$: low loss, but not zero.) Numerical dispersion adds a smaller error.
(d) For $\sigma\gg\omega\varepsilon_0$ (a good conductor), $k\approx(1 + i)\sqrt{\mu_0\sigma\omega/2}$: the wave decays within a **skin depth** $\delta = \sqrt{2/\mu_0\sigma\omega}$, moves far slower than $c$, and is strongly dispersive (EM-7). In FDTD, $c_a\to-1$, and the grid must resolve $\delta$ with several cells.

**C3.** (a) $\mathcal L = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}|\nabla V + \partial_t\mathbf A|^2 - \frac{1}{2\mu_0}|\nabla\times\mathbf A|^2 - \rho V + \mathbf J\cdot\mathbf A$. The Euler–Lagrange equation for the field $A_i$ is $\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial A_i} - \partial_t\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_tA_i)} - \partial_j\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_jA_i)} = 0$. The pieces:
1. $\partial\mathcal L/\partial A_i = J_i$.
2. $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_tA_i) = \varepsilon_0(\partial_iV + \partial_tA_i) = -\varepsilon_0E_i$.
3. $B_k = \varepsilon_{klm}\partial_lA_m$, so $\partial(B^2)/\partial(\partial_jA_i) = 2B_k\varepsilon_{kji}$ and $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_jA_i) = -\mu_0^{-1}\varepsilon_{kji}B_k$.
4. Assemble: $J_i + \varepsilon_0\partial_tE_i + \mu_0^{-1}\varepsilon_{kji}\partial_jB_k = 0$. Since $\varepsilon_{kji} = -\varepsilon_{ijk}$, the last term is $-\mu_0^{-1}(\nabla\times\mathbf B)_i$. So $\nabla\times\mathbf B = \mu_0\mathbf J + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf E$.

(b) $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$ are gauge invariant, so only the source terms change: $-\rho V' + \mathbf J\cdot\mathbf A' = -\rho V + \mathbf J\cdot\mathbf A + \rho\,\partial_t\lambda + \mathbf J\cdot\nabla\lambda$, and
$$\rho\,\partial_t\lambda + \mathbf J\cdot\nabla\lambda = \partial_t(\rho\lambda) + \nabla\cdot(\lambda\mathbf J) - \lambda(\partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot\mathbf J).$$
The first two terms integrate to boundary terms in the action $\int\mathcal L\,d^3x\,dt$ and do not affect the field equations. The last vanishes for every $\lambda$ if and only if $\partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot\mathbf J = 0$. A gauge-invariant theory can only couple to a conserved current.
(c) $\mathcal L$ contains $\partial_t\mathbf A$ but never $\partial_tV$ ($\mathbf E = -\nabla V - \partial_t\mathbf A$). So $V$'s Euler–Lagrange equation, Gauss's law, has no second time derivative: it is a constraint that fixes $V$ at each instant from $\rho$ and $\mathbf A$, not an equation of motion. $V$ is not an independent dynamical variable. Together with the gauge freedom this leaves two true degrees of freedom per point (the two transverse polarizations of light), not four.
