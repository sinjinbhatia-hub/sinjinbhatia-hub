## A. Warm-up

**A1.** (a) $\nabla^2f = \partial_x^2(x^2) + \partial_y^2(y^2) + \partial_z^2(-2z^2) = 2 + 2 - 4 = 0$. Write a point of the sphere as $\mathbf r_0 + R_s\hat{\mathbf n}$ with $\hat{\mathbf n} = (n_1, n_2, n_3) = (\sin u\cos v, \sin u\sin v, \cos u)$. Then
$$f = (x_0 + R_sn_1)^2 + (y_0 + R_sn_2)^2 - 2(z_0 + R_sn_3)^2 = f(\mathbf r_0) + 2R_s\,(x_0n_1 + y_0n_2 - 2z_0n_3) + R_s^2\,(n_1^2 + n_2^2 - 2n_3^2).$$
Average over the sphere with weight $\sin u\,du\,dv/4\pi$. Each $\langle n_i\rangle = 0$ (the $v$ integral kills $n_1, n_2$; $\int_0^\pi\cos u\sin u\,du = 0$ kills $n_3$). Each $\langle n_i^2\rangle = \tfrac13$: for $n_3$, $\frac1{4\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}dv\int_0^\pi\cos^2u\sin u\,du = \frac{2\pi}{4\pi}\cdot\frac23 = \frac13$, and the three add to $\langle|\hat{\mathbf n}|^2\rangle = 1$ with equal shares by symmetry. Cross terms $\langle n_in_j\rangle$ vanish because of the $v$ integral ($\cos v\sin v$, $\cos v$, $\sin v$ all average to zero). So
$$\langle f\rangle = f(\mathbf r_0) + R_s^2\left(\tfrac13 + \tfrac13 - \tfrac23\right) = f(\mathbf r_0),$$
which is Theorem 3.1 for this $f$.

(b) For $g = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$ the same expansion gives $\langle g\rangle = g(\mathbf r_0) + R_s^2\left(\tfrac13 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac13\right) = g(\mathbf r_0) + R_s^2$. Here $\nabla^2g = 6 > 0$, and the excess is $R_s^2 = \tfrac{R_s^2}{6}\nabla^2g$: a positive Laplacian means the function curves upward on average, so its neighbours sit above the centre value and the sphere average exceeds $g(\mathbf r_0)$. For a potential, $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$, so at a point inside *positive* charge the average of $V$ on a small sphere is *below* $V$ at the centre: a local maximum of $V$ is possible exactly where positive charge sits, which is why Earnshaw's theorem applies only to a test charge in a charge-free region. (Part 0 computes both sphere averages symbolically.)

## B. Core

**B1.** (a) Let $\mathcal V$ be the region outside the conductors (and inside a large sphere $S_\infty$). Green's second identity, EM-1 (3.23), with $\varphi = V$, $\psi = V'$:
$$\int_{\mathcal V}\left(V\nabla^2V' - V'\nabla^2V\right)d\tau = \oint_{S_\infty}(V\nabla V' - V'\nabla V)\cdot d\mathbf a + \sum_i\oint_{S_i}(V\nabla V' - V'\nabla V)\cdot d\mathbf a,$$
with $d\mathbf a$ pointing out of $\mathcal V$, i.e. *into* each conductor $i$. Poisson's equation turns the left side into $\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal V}\left(-V\rho' + V'\rho\right)d\tau$. On $S_\infty$, $V, V'\sim1/r$ and $\nabla V, \nabla V'\sim1/r^2$ for localized charge, so the integrand is $O(r^{-3})$ over an area $O(r^2)$ and the term vanishes. On conductor $i$, $V = V_i$ and $V' = V'_i$ are constants (EM-2 Theorem 3.4(iv)) and come outside: $V_i\oint_{S_i}\nabla V'\cdot d\mathbf a - V'_i\oint_{S_i}\nabla V\cdot d\mathbf a$. With $\nabla V = -\mathbf E$ and $d\mathbf a$ pointing into the conductor, $\oint_{S_i}\nabla V\cdot d\mathbf a = +\oint_{S_i}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf a_{\text{out}} = Q_i/\varepsilon_0$ by Gauss's law (the surface encloses the conductor's charge $Q_i$), and likewise $Q'_i/\varepsilon_0$ for the primed system. Multiplying through by $\varepsilon_0$:
$$\int_{\mathcal V}\rho\,V'\,d\tau + \sum_iQ_iV'_i = \int_{\mathcal V}\rho'\,V\,d\tau + \sum_iQ'_iV_i. \tag{B1}$$
This is Green's reciprocity theorem: "charges of system 1 times potentials of system 2, summed" equals the same with $1\leftrightarrow2$. If the conductors' surface charges are counted as part of $\rho$ and $\rho'$, the sums are absorbed into the integrals and it reads $\int\rho V' = \int\rho'V$. ∎

(b) System 1: $q$ at height $d$, plane grounded ($V_{\text{pl}} = 0$), induced charge $Q_{\text{ind}}$ on the plane. System 2: no point charge, plane at $V_0$. Its potential in the upper half-space is $V' = V_0$ everywhere (it satisfies Laplace's equation and the boundary value; uniqueness, Theorem 3.3, applied to the half-space closed by a large hemisphere on which $V' = V_0$). So $\mathbf E' = 0$ and the plane carries $Q'_{\text{pl}} = 0$. The $S_\infty$ term still vanishes: $\nabla V' = 0$ and $V'\nabla V\sim V_0\times(\text{dipole field})\sim r^{-3}$. Put everything into (B1): left side $qV'(\mathbf r_q) + Q_{\text{ind}}V'_{\text{pl}} = qV_0 + Q_{\text{ind}}V_0$; right side $0 + Q'_{\text{pl}}V_{\text{pl}} = 0$. Hence $Q_{\text{ind}} = -q$, with no integral over $\sigma$.

(c) System 1: $q$ at distance $a$ from the centre, sphere grounded, induced charge $Q_{\text{ind}}$. System 2: the sphere alone at potential $V_0$, so $V' = V_0R/r$ outside (charge $Q' = V_0R/k$ on it). (B1): $qV'(a) + Q_{\text{ind}}V'_{\text{sph}} = Q'V_{\text{sph}}$, i.e. $qV_0R/a + Q_{\text{ind}}V_0 = Q'\cdot0$, so $Q_{\text{ind}} = -qR/a$. This is $q'$ of (3.6), obtained without knowing where the induced charge sits.

**B2.** (a) With $\theta$ measured from the axis through $q$, a point of the sphere is at distance $d_1 = \sqrt{a^2 + R^2 - 2aR\cos\theta}$ from $q$ and $d_2 = \sqrt{b^2 + R^2 - 2bR\cos\theta}$ from $q'$. With $b = R^2/a$,
$$d_2^2 = \frac{R^4}{a^2} + R^2 - \frac{2R^3\cos\theta}{a} = \frac{R^2}{a^2}\left(R^2 + a^2 - 2aR\cos\theta\right) = \left(\frac Ra\right)^2d_1^2,$$
so $d_2 = (R/a)\,d_1$ for every $\theta$, and $V = k\left[\dfrac q{d_1} + \dfrac{-qR/a}{(R/a)d_1}\right] = 0$ on the whole sphere. (Part 2 checks $|V| < 10^{-13}$ V at 1000 random points.)

(b) For $r\ge R$, $V(r,\theta) = kq\left[\left(r^2 + a^2 - 2ar\cos\theta\right)^{-1/2} - \dfrac Ra\left(r^2 + b^2 - 2br\cos\theta\right)^{-1/2}\right]$. Differentiate: $\partial_r\left(r^2 + c^2 - 2cr\cos\theta\right)^{-1/2} = -(r - c\cos\theta)\left(r^2 + c^2 - 2cr\cos\theta\right)^{-3/2}$. At $r = R$ write $D = R^2 + a^2 - 2aR\cos\theta$; the second root is $(R/a)^3D^{3/2}$ by (a). Then
$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\Big|_R = kq\left[-\frac{R - a\cos\theta}{D^{3/2}} + \frac Ra\cdot\frac{R - (R^2/a)\cos\theta}{(R/a)^3D^{3/2}}\right] = \frac{kq}{D^{3/2}}\left[-R + a\cos\theta + \frac{a^2}{R^2}\left(R - \frac{R^2}a\cos\theta\right)\right] = \frac{kq}{D^{3/2}}\cdot\frac{a^2 - R^2}{R}.$$
So, with $\hat{\mathbf n} = \hat{\mathbf r}$ out of the metal,
$$\sigma(\theta) = -\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\Big|_R = -\frac{q\,(a^2 - R^2)}{4\pi R\left(R^2 + a^2 - 2aR\cos\theta\right)^{3/2}}.$$
Negative everywhere, largest at $\theta = 0$. Total: $Q = \int_0^\pi\sigma\,2\pi R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta = -\dfrac{q(a^2 - R^2)R}{2}\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1\frac{du}{(R^2 + a^2 - 2aRu)^{3/2}}$ with $u = \cos\theta$. Substitute $w = R^2 + a^2 - 2aRu$, $dw = -2aR\,du$:
$$\int_{-1}^1\frac{du}{(\cdots)^{3/2}} = \frac{1}{2aR}\int_{(a-R)^2}^{(a+R)^2}w^{-3/2}\,dw = \frac{1}{2aR}\left[-2w^{-1/2}\right]_{(a-R)^2}^{(a+R)^2} = \frac1{aR}\left(\frac1{a-R} - \frac1{a+R}\right) = \frac{2}{a(a^2 - R^2)}.$$
Hence $Q = -\dfrac{q(a^2 - R^2)R}{2}\cdot\dfrac{2}{a(a^2 - R^2)} = -\dfrac{qR}{a} = q'$. ✓ For Example 4.2's numbers ($R = 10$ cm, $a = 20$ cm, $q = 1$ nC) Part 2 prints $\sigma(0) = -23.873$ nC/m², $\sigma(\pi) = -0.8842$ nC/m², and the integral $-0.500000$ nC.

(c) A neutral isolated sphere must still be an equipotential, and must carry total charge zero. The pair $q, q'$ makes the sphere an equipotential at $V = 0$ but gives it charge $q' = -qR/a$. Add a second image $q'' = +qR/a$ **at the centre**: it adds the constant $kq''/R$ on the sphere (so the sphere stays an equipotential), it restores the total charge to $q' + q'' = 0$, and it lies outside the region of interest. By Theorem 3.4 (total charge on the conductor given), this is *the* exterior field. The sphere's potential is $V_{\text{sph}} = kq''/R = kq/a$, which is also the potential $q$ alone produces at the centre, as the mean-value property demands for an uncharged conductor (its potential is the average of the external potential over its surface). The force on $q$:
$$F = kq\left[\frac{q'}{(a - b)^2} + \frac{q''}{a^2}\right] = -\frac{kq^2Ra}{(a^2 - R^2)^2} + \frac{kq^2R}{a^3} = -kq^2R\,\frac{a^4 - (a^2 - R^2)^2}{a^3(a^2 - R^2)^2} = -\frac{kq^2R^3\,(2a^2 - R^2)}{a^3\,(a^2 - R^2)^2}.$$
Always attractive. Far away ($a\gg R$): $F\to-2kq^2R^3/a^5$, the force on $q$ from the dipole $p = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3E_0$ it induces (Example 4.4 with $E_0 = kq/a^2$). Near contact ($a\to R$): $F\to-kq^2/4(a-R)^2$, the same as the grounded sphere. Part 2 asserts this closed form against the two-image sum for $a = 1.001R, 1.01R, 10R$ and prints $F = -8.7379\times10^{-8}$ N for Example 4.2's $a = 2R$ (grounded: $-1.9972\times10^{-7}$ N).

(d) $R = 10$ cm, $q = 1$ nC (Part 2, the "B2(d)" lines):

| $a$ | grounded $F$ | $F/\left[-kq^2/4(a-R)^2\right]$ | neutral $F$ | $F/\left[-2kq^2R^3/a^5\right]$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $1.001R$ | $-0.2247$ N | $1.0000$ | $-0.2247$ N | $1.3\times10^5$ |
| $1.01R$ | $-2.2468\times10^{-3}$ N | $1.0000$ | $-2.2460\times10^{-3}$ N | $1313$ |

At $a = 1.001R$ and $1.01R$ both spheres are in the plane limit: the force is set by the gap $a - R$, and whether the far side of the sphere carries $+qR/a$ or nothing is irrelevant. At $10R$ the grounded sphere is nowhere near the plane formula (it is in its own far limit $-kq^2R/a^3$, a Coulomb force from $q' = -qR/a$) while the neutral sphere has reached its induced-dipole limit to $1.5\%$. The neutral force diverges near contact because the induced $-qR/a$ sits at $b\to R$, a distance $a - R\to0$ from $q$, while the compensating $+qR/a$ sits at the centre, a distance $R$ away: the near charge wins by the factor $R^2/(a-R)^2$. Neutral overall does not mean neutral locally.

**B3.** (a) The $y$ conditions ($V = 0$ at $y = 0, a$) are as in §3.4 steps 1–3: $Y = \sin(n\pi y/a)$, separation constant $k^2 = (n\pi/a)^2$, and $X'' = k^2X$, so $X = Ce^{kx} + De^{-kx}$. In the slot we killed $C$ with the condition at $x\to\infty$. Here the region is bounded, $0 < x < b$, so neither exponential can be excluded by growth; instead the grounded wall at $x = 0$ demands $X(0) = 0$, i.e. $C + D = 0$, and $X\propto e^{kx} - e^{-kx}\propto\sinh(kx)$. That is why $\sinh$ appears: it is the combination of the two exponentials that vanishes on the grounded wall. Superposing,
$$V(x,y) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty C_n\sinh\frac{n\pi x}{a}\sin\frac{n\pi y}{a},\qquad C_n\sinh\frac{n\pi b}{a} = \frac2a\int_0^aV_0\sin\frac{n\pi y}a\,dy = \begin{cases}4V_0/n\pi & n\text{ odd}\\0 & n\text{ even,}\end{cases}$$
which is (3.10) with constant data: $V = \dfrac{4V_0}{\pi}\displaystyle\sum_{n\text{ odd}}\frac1n\,\frac{\sinh(n\pi x/a)}{\sinh(n\pi b/a)}\sin\frac{n\pi y}{a}$.

(b) $b = a$, centre $(a/2, a/2)$: $\sin(n\pi/2) = (-1)^{(n-1)/2}$ for odd $n$ and $\dfrac{\sinh(n\pi/2)}{\sinh(n\pi)} = \dfrac{1}{2\cosh(n\pi/2)}$ (double-angle formula), so the $n$-th term is $\dfrac{2\,(-1)^{(n-1)/2}}{n\pi\cosh(n\pi/2)}$. $n = 1$: $2/(\pi\times2.50918) = 0.253716$. $n = 3$: $-2/(3\pi\times55.6634) = -0.003812$, sum $0.249904$. $n = 5$: $+9.9\times10^{-5}$, sum $0.250003$. $n = 7$: $-3\times10^{-6}$, sum $0.250000$. (Part 3, the "B3" line.) Each term is down by about $e^{-\pi}\approx1/23$ from the last.

(c) Consider the four problems in which exactly one wall of the square pipe is at $V_0$ and the other three are grounded. The square's rotational symmetry maps each onto the next, and the centre onto itself, so all four have the same centre value $V_c$. Their sum is harmonic and equals $V_0$ on all four walls, so by Theorem 3.3 it is $V\equiv V_0$. Hence $4V_c = V_0$ and $V_c = V_0/4$ exactly, which is what the series in (b) converges to.

**B4.** (a) $\ell = 3$, odd series, $a_1 = 1$. From (3.14) with $k = 1$: $a_3 = \dfrac{(1-3)(1+3+1)}{2\cdot3}a_1 = \dfrac{(-2)(5)}{6} = -\dfrac53$. With $k = 3$: $a_5 = \dfrac{(3-3)(3+3+1)}{4\cdot5}a_3 = 0$, and every later coefficient is zero. So $P\propto x - \tfrac53x^3$, which at $x = 1$ equals $-\tfrac23$. Dividing by $-\tfrac23$: $P_3 = \tfrac12(5x^3 - 3x)$, matching §3.5 and Part 0's `P[3]`.

(b) $\ell = 1$, even series, $a_0 = 1$. The ratio in (3.14) is $\dfrac{a_{k+2}}{a_k} = \dfrac{(k-1)(k+2)}{(k+1)(k+2)} = \dfrac{k-1}{k+1}$. So $a_2 = \dfrac{-1}{1}a_0 = -1$, $a_4 = \dfrac13a_2 = -\dfrac13$, $a_6 = \dfrac35a_4 = -\dfrac15$, and the product telescopes: $a_{2m} = -\dfrac{1}{2m-1}$ for $m\ge1$. The series is
$$1 - x^2 - \frac{x^4}3 - \frac{x^6}5 - \cdots = 1 - x\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{x^{2m-1}}{2m-1} = 1 - x\operatorname{artanh}x\qquad(|x|<1),$$
since $\operatorname{artanh}x = x + x^3/3 + x^5/5 + \cdots$. (Part 0 asserts the series through $x^{14}$ against SymPy's expansion of $1 - x\operatorname{artanh}x$.) At $x\to\pm1$, $\operatorname{artanh}x = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1+x}{1-x}\to\pm\infty$ logarithmically: this is the $C/k$ coefficient decay and $\tfrac C2\ln N$ divergence of §3.5 steps 3–4, with $ka_k\to C = -1$. Up to sign this is the Legendre function of the second kind, $Q_1(x) = x\operatorname{artanh}x - 1$. It is rejected for a sphere because the poles $x = \pm1$ (the whole $z$ axis) lie inside the region and carry no charge, so $V$ must be finite there. If the region excluded the axis (the space between two coaxial cones, or outside a thin wire along $z$), the $Q_\ell$ would be admissible, the boundary conditions on the cones would fix $\ell$, and $\ell$ would in general not be an integer.

**B5.** (a) Superpose Example 4.4 with a point charge $Q$ at the centre:
$$V(r,\theta) = -E_0\left(r - \frac{R^3}{r^2}\right)\cos\theta + \frac{kQ}{r}\qquad(r\ge R).$$
Both pieces are harmonic outside the sphere; at infinity $V\to-E_0z$ as required; on $r = R$, $V = kQ/R$, a constant, so the sphere is an equipotential; and the total charge on the sphere is $Q$ (the $\cos\theta$ piece carries zero net charge, Example 4.4). Theorem 3.4, the conductor version of uniqueness, says the field outside is fixed by the field at infinity and the *total* charge on the conductor, so this is the solution, and we never had to know the sphere's potential in advance (it comes out as $kQ/R$).

**B7.** (a) $\mathbf E = kQ\,\mathbf r/r^3$. Apply $(\mathbf p\cdot\nabla)$ with $\mathbf p$ constant: $(\mathbf p\cdot\nabla)\mathbf r = \mathbf p$ and $(\mathbf p\cdot\nabla)r^{-3} = -3r^{-4}\,(\mathbf p\cdot\nabla)r = -3r^{-4}\,(\mathbf p\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})$. So

(c) The product rule for $\nabla(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E)$ is $(\mathbf p\cdot\nabla)\mathbf E + (\mathbf E\cdot\nabla)\mathbf p + \mathbf p\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E) + \mathbf E\times(\nabla\times\mathbf p)$. For a rigid permanent dipole $\mathbf p$ is a constant vector and the two terms with derivatives of $\mathbf p$ vanish; for an electrostatic field $\nabla\times\mathbf E = 0$; then $\mathbf F = (\mathbf p\cdot\nabla)\mathbf E = \nabla(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E) = -\nabla U$. $$+q\ \text{at}\ z = z_0 + 2nL,\qquad -q\ \text{at}\ z = -z_0 + 2nL.$$
On $z = 0$: the charge $+q$ at $z_0 + 2nL$ and the charge $-q$ at $-z_0 - 2nL$ (the $-q$ family's member with index $-n$) are mirror images in $z = 0$ with opposite signs, so their potentials cancel at every point of the plane; as $n\mapsto-n$ is a bijection of the index set, the whole series cancels and $V = 0$. On $z = L$: pair $+q$ at $z_0 + 2nL$ with $-q$ at $-z_0 + 2(1 - n)L$; their positions sum to $2L$, so they are mirror images in $z = L$ with opposite signs, and $n\mapsto1 - n$ is again a bijection. $V = 0$ there too. **C2.** Write $D = 1 - 2xt + t^2$, so $G = D^{-1/2}$. Since $D = (t - x)^2 + (1 - x^2) > 0$ for real $t$ when $|x| < 1$, and $D$ vanishes at $t = x\pm i\sqrt{1 - x^2}$, which have $|t| = 1$, $G$ is analytic in $t$ for $|t| < 1$ and the series $\sum c_\ell(x)t^\ell$ converges there.

(a) $\dfrac{\partial G}{\partial t} = -\tfrac12D^{-3/2}\,(2t - 2x) = (x - t)D^{-3/2}$, so $D\,\partial_tG = (x - t)D^{-1/2} = (x - t)G$. ✓ Insert the series: $\partial_tG = \sum\ell c_\ell t^{\ell-1}$, and
$$(1 - 2xt + t^2)\sum_\ell\ell c_\ell t^{\ell-1} = (x - t)\sum_\ell c_\ell t^\ell.$$
The coefficient of $t^\ell$ on the left is $(\ell+1)c_{\ell+1} - 2x\ell c_\ell + (\ell-1)c_{\ell-1}$; on the right, $xc_\ell - c_{\ell-1}$. Equating, $(\ell+1)c_{\ell+1} = (2\ell+1)x\,c_\ell - \ell\,c_{\ell-1}$, which is Bonnet's recursion (3.18). Initial values: $c_0 = G(x, 0) = 1$ and $c_1 = \partial_tG|_{t=0} = x$. By induction on the recursion, $c_\ell$ is a polynomial in $x$ of degree exactly $\ell$ with leading coefficient $\frac{(2\ell-1)(2\ell-3)\cdots1}{\ell!}$ and parity $(-1)^\ell$. Also $c_\ell(1) = 1$ for all $\ell$: at $x = 1$, $G = (1 - t)^{-1} = \sum t^\ell$. The recursion with $c_0 = 1$, $c_1 = x$ generates one definite sequence of polynomials; part (b) identifies it with $P_\ell$.

(b) Compute the two pieces. First, $\partial_xG = tD^{-3/2}$, so $(1 - x^2)\partial_xG = t(1 - x^2)D^{-3/2}$ and
$$\partial_x\left[(1 - x^2)\partial_xG\right] = t\left[-2xD^{-3/2} + 3t(1 - x^2)D^{-5/2}\right] = tD^{-5/2}\left[-2xD + 3t(1 - x^2)\right] = tD^{-5/2}\left[-2x + 3t + x^2t - 2xt^2\right].$$
Second, $tG = tD^{-1/2}$; $\partial_t(tG) = D^{-1/2} + t(x - t)D^{-3/2} = D^{-3/2}\left[D + xt - t^2\right] = (1 - xt)D^{-3/2}$; then
$$\partial_t^2(tG) = -xD^{-3/2} + (1 - xt)\cdot3(x - t)D^{-5/2} = D^{-5/2}\left[-xD + 3(x - t)(1 - xt)\right] = D^{-5/2}\left[2x - 3t - x^2t + 2xt^2\right],$$
using $-xD = -x + 2x^2t - xt^2$ and $3(x - t)(1 - xt) = 3x - 3t - 3x^2t + 3xt^2$. Multiply by $t$ and add to the first piece: the brackets are negatives of each other, so
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[(1 - x^2)\frac{\partial G}{\partial x}\right] + t\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}\big(tG\big) = 0.\ ✓$$
(Part 0 verifies both identities of (a) and (b) with SymPy.) Insert $G = \sum c_\ell t^\ell$: the first term is $\sum_\ell t^\ell\,\frac{d}{dx}\left[(1 - x^2)c_\ell'\right]$; the second is $t\,\partial_t^2\sum_\ell c_\ell t^{\ell+1} = \sum_\ell(\ell+1)\ell\,c_\ell t^\ell$. The coefficient of $t^\ell$ must vanish:
$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[(1 - x^2)\frac{dc_\ell}{dx}\right] + \ell(\ell+1)\,c_\ell = 0,$$
Legendre's equation (3.13) with integer $\ell$. Now identify: by §3.5, for integer $\ell$ the solutions of (3.13) are spanned by one polynomial ($P_\ell$) and one series that diverges at $x = \pm1$ (the $Q_\ell$ of B4(b)); a polynomial solution must be a multiple of $P_\ell$. Since $c_\ell$ is a polynomial with $c_\ell(1) = 1 = P_\ell(1)$, $c_\ell = P_\ell$. This also proves Bonnet's recursion (3.18) for the Legendre polynomials themselves, closing the loop left open in §3.6, and shows that the coefficients of the generating function are exactly the polynomials the Frobenius method produced.

At $1$ nm and beyond, $U_0\ll k_BT$ and the leading term is exact to a part in $10^3$ or better. (At $0.5$ nm the point-dipole model of a $0.3$ nm molecule is itself questionable, but that is a separate failure.)

(d) The interaction energy of two fixed dipoles scales as $1/r^3$ and averages to zero; the *thermal* average is second order in $U_0$ because the Boltzmann weight tilts the orientation distribution toward the attractive configurations by an amount $\propto U_0/k_BT$, and the energy gained is $U_0$ times that tilt: $\langle U\rangle\propto-U_0^2/k_BT\propto-p^4/(k_BT\,r^6)$. This is the **Keesom** term: permanent dipole with permanent dipole, orientation-averaged, inversely proportional to $T$ and vanishing for nonpolar molecules. The **Debye** term is permanent with induced: the field $E\sim kp/r^3$ of a permanent dipole induces $\mathbf p_{\text{ind}} = \alpha\mathbf E$ in a neighbour, with energy $-\tfrac12\alpha E^2\propto-\alpha p^2/r^6$ for *every* orientation (no averaging, no $T$ dependence, since an induced dipole always points along the field and always attracts). The **London** term is induced with induced: quantum-mechanical fluctuations of the charge in one molecule produce an instantaneous dipole whose field polarizes the other, with energy $\propto-\hbar\omega_0\alpha^2/r^6$ ($\omega_0$ a characteristic electronic frequency); it exists even between noble-gas atoms, is independent of $T$, and is usually the largest of the three. All three go as $1/r^6$ because each is "a $1/r^3$ dipole field, squared"; their sum is the $-C/r^6$ attraction in the Lennard-Jones potential and the $a/V^2$ of van der Waals' equation, which SM-6 uses to build a gas that condenses.
