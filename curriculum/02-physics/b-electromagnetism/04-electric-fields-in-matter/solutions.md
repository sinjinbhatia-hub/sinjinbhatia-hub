# EM-4 Solutions

Conventions as in the notes: $k = 1/4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 8.988\times10^9$ N m²/C², $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}$ F/m, $\hat{\mathbf n}$ out of the dielectric for $\sigma_b$. Each number below is printed or asserted by `project.py`; the part is named where it appears (Part 1 for the bound-charge exercises, Part 7 for the "Exercise numbers" block).

## A. Warm-up

**A1.** (a) By (3.2), $\alpha/4\pi\varepsilon_0 = a^3$, so $a = (0.667\text{ Å}^3)^{1/3} = 0.8737$ Å, which is $1.65$ times the Bohr radius $0.529$ Å: the model's cloud radius is a fit parameter of the right size, not the Bohr radius.

(b) $p = \alpha E = 4\pi\varepsilon_0(0.667\times10^{-30}\text{ m}^3)(10^6\text{ V/m}) = 1.1127\times10^{-10}\times6.67\times10^{-31}\times10^6 = 7.421\times10^{-35}$ C m. The displacement is $d = p/e = 7.421\times10^{-35}/1.602\times10^{-19} = 4.632\times10^{-16}$ m: about half a proton radius, and $5.3\times10^{-6}$ of the atom's.

(c) $E_a = \dfrac{e}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a^2} = \dfrac{8.988\times10^9\times1.602\times10^{-19}}{(0.8737\times10^{-10})^2} = \dfrac{1.440\times10^{-9}}{7.633\times10^{-21}} = 1.886\times10^{11}$ V/m. From step 2 of §3.1, $d = 4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3E/q$, so $d/a = 4\pi\varepsilon_0a^2E/e = E/E_a = 10^6/1.886\times10^{11} = 5.30\times10^{-6}$ ✓ (the same number as $d/a = 4.632\times10^{-16}/0.8737\times10^{-10}$). The linear law $\mathbf p = \alpha\mathbf E$ holds because $d/a$ is tiny: the spring is harmonic only for $d\ll a$. It must fail as $E\to E_a$ (about $10^{11}$ V/m, the field of a focused pulsed laser), where the atom is pulled apart: then $\mathbf p$ has terms in $E^2$, $E^3$ (the hyperpolarizabilities behind nonlinear optics) and finally ionization. Part 7, "A1" line.

**A2.** By (3.8), $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf P = -\partial_x(kx) = -k = -10^{-4}$ C/m³, uniform. Faces: at $x = L$, $\hat{\mathbf n} = +\hat{\mathbf x}$, $\sigma_b = kL = 2\times10^{-6}$ C/m². At $x = 0$, $\hat{\mathbf n} = -\hat{\mathbf x}$ but $\mathbf P = 0$, so $\sigma_b = 0$. At $y = 0, L$ and $z = 0, L$, $\hat{\mathbf n}\perp\hat{\mathbf x}\parallel\mathbf P$, so $\mathbf P\cdot\hat{\mathbf n} = 0$: no bound charge, because no dipole chain pokes through a face that is parallel to the chains. Totals: $Q_{\text{vol}} = \rho_bL^3 = -10^{-4}\times8\times10^{-6} = -8\times10^{-10}$ C and $Q_{\text{surf}} = \sigma_bL^2 = 2\times10^{-6}\times4\times10^{-4} = +8\times10^{-10}$ C, sum zero ✓ (3.10). The volume charge is negative because $\mathbf P$ grows with $x$: each slice sends out more dipole moment than it receives. (Part 1, "A2" line.)

**A3.** (a) $N_{\max} = pE = 6.17\times10^{-30}\times10^5 = 6.17\times10^{-25}$ N m, at $\theta = 90°$.
(b) $k_BT = 1.3806\times10^{-23}\times300 = 4.142\times10^{-21}$ J, so $2pE/k_BT = 1.234\times10^{-24}/4.142\times10^{-21} = 2.98\times10^{-4}$ ✓ (the $3\times10^{-4}$ quoted in §3.2).
(c) $2pE/k_BT = 1$ at $E = k_BT/2p = 4.142\times10^{-21}/1.234\times10^{-29} = 3.357\times10^8$ V/m, which is $112$ times air's breakdown field. So no laboratory field comes near saturating the alignment: the Langevin average is $\langle\cos\theta\rangle\approx x/3$ with $x = pE/k_BT = 1.49\times10^{-4}$, i.e. $4.97\times10^{-5}$, and $P\propto E$ is linear (this is why a linear dielectric exists at all, and why Debye's orientational term is $p^2/3k_BT$). Part 7, "A3" line.

**A4.** $C = \varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0A/d = 2.1\times8.854\times10^{-12}\times5\times10^{-3}/10^{-3} = 92.97$ pF. $Q = CV = 9.297$ nC, $\sigma_f = Q/A = 1.859\times10^{-6}$ C/m². $E = V/d = 10^5$ V/m (uniform; $V = Ed$). $P = \varepsilon_0\chi_eE = 8.854\times10^{-12}\times1.1\times10^5 = 9.740\times10^{-7}$ C/m². The dielectric face touching the positive plate has outward normal $-\hat{\mathbf y}$ (toward the plate) while $\mathbf P$ points along $\mathbf E$, away from the positive plate, so $\sigma_b = -P = -9.740\times10^{-7}$ C/m². Then $\sigma_f + \sigma_b = 1.859\times10^{-6} - 9.740\times10^{-7} = 8.854\times10^{-7} = \varepsilon_0E$ ✓ ($\varepsilon_0\times10^5 = 8.854\times10^{-7}$). Meaning: Gauss's law for $\mathbf E$ at the plate sees all charge, free and bound; the bound charge cancels $1 - 1/\varepsilon_r = 52\%$ of the free charge. Also $D = \sigma_f = \varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0E$ ✓. Part 7, "A4" line.

**A5.** (a) No free charge, so (3.16) gives $\varepsilon_1E_1\cos\theta_1 = \varepsilon_2E_2\cos\theta_2$ (normal $\mathbf D$), and (3.17) gives $E_1\sin\theta_1 = E_2\sin\theta_2$ (tangential $\mathbf E$). Divide the second by the first: $\dfrac{\tan\theta_1}{\varepsilon_1} = \dfrac{\tan\theta_2}{\varepsilon_2}$, i.e. $\tan\theta_2/\tan\theta_1 = \varepsilon_2/\varepsilon_1$.
(b) $\tan\theta_2 = 5\tan30° = 2.8868$, $\theta_2 = 70.89°$. From the tangential condition $|\mathbf E_2|/|\mathbf E_1| = \sin30°/\sin70.89° = 0.5/0.9449 = 0.5292$.
(c) Away from the normal: lines lie closer to the interface in the high-$\varepsilon$ medium. Read the other way, $\tan\theta_1 = (\varepsilon_1/\varepsilon_2)\tan\theta_2\to0$ as $\varepsilon_2\to\infty$: on the vacuum side the lines arrive perpendicular to the surface, as at a conductor (EM-2 §3.9), and inside the dielectric $E^\perp = D/\varepsilon\to0$. Part 7, "A5" line.

## B. Core

**B1.** (a) For a radial field $\nabla\cdot\mathbf P = r^{-2}\partial_r(r^2P_r)$, so $\rho_b = -r^{-2}\partial_r(kr^3) = -3k$. At $r = R$, $\hat{\mathbf n} = \hat{\mathbf r}$: $\sigma_b = kR$. Totals: $Q_{\text{vol}} = -3k\cdot\tfrac43\pi R^3 = -4\pi kR^3$, $Q_{\text{surf}} = kR\cdot4\pi R^2 = +4\pi kR^3$, sum zero ✓. (SymPy in Part 1 repeats this.)

(b) Gauss's law with the bound charge (EM-2 (3.5)), sphere of radius $r<R$: $4\pi r^2E = \dfrac1{\varepsilon_0}(-3k)\dfrac43\pi r^3 = -\dfrac{4\pi kr^3}{\varepsilon_0}$, so $\mathbf E = -\dfrac{kr}{\varepsilon_0}\hat{\mathbf r}$ for $r<R$. For $r>R$ the enclosed bound charge is $Q_b = 0$, so $\mathbf E = 0$.

(c) Free charge zero and spherical symmetry: (3.15) gives $4\pi r^2D = 0$, so $\mathbf D = 0$ everywhere. Then $\varepsilon_0\mathbf E = -\mathbf P$: $\mathbf E = -kr\hat{\mathbf r}/\varepsilon_0$ inside and $0$ outside ($\mathbf P = 0$ there). Same answer ✓. The second route is the one-line version: $\mathbf D$ is fixed by free charge, so with none, $\mathbf E = -\mathbf P/\varepsilon_0$.

(d) $\rho_b = -3\times10^{-4}$ C/m³, $\sigma_b = kR = 10^{-6}$ C/m², $E_{\text{in}}(R) = -kR/\varepsilon_0 = -10^{-6}/8.854\times10^{-12} = -1.1294\times10^5$ V/m. The jump in $E_r$ across $R$ is $0 - (-1.1294\times10^5) = +1.1294\times10^5 = \sigma_b/\varepsilon_0$ ✓: (3.16) with $\sigma_f = 0$ says $D^\perp$ is continuous, so $\varepsilon_0\,\Delta E^\perp = -\Delta P^\perp = P(R) = \sigma_b$. Part 1, "B1" line.

**B2.** (a) With $\mathbf P = 0$ outside, take a sphere $S_\infty$ enclosing the object. $\int\rho_b\,d\tau = -\int\nabla\cdot\mathbf P\,d\tau = -\oint_{S_\infty}\mathbf P\cdot d\mathbf a = 0$, because $\mathbf P = 0$ on $S_\infty$. The divergence theorem is valid across the jump in $\mathbf P$ at the object's surface provided $\nabla\cdot\mathbf P$ includes the delta-function layer $-\sigma_b\delta$ there; written without distributions this is exactly (3.10): $-\int_{\mathcal V}\nabla\cdot\mathbf P + \oint\mathbf P\cdot d\mathbf a = 0$.

(b) The $i$th component: $\int x_i\rho_b\,d\tau = -\int x_i\nabla\cdot\mathbf P\,d\tau$. Use $\nabla\cdot(x_i\mathbf P) = P_i + x_i\nabla\cdot\mathbf P$ (product rule, $\nabla x_i = \hat{\mathbf x}_i$): $-\int x_i\nabla\cdot\mathbf P\,d\tau = \int P_i\,d\tau - \int\nabla\cdot(x_i\mathbf P)\,d\tau = \int P_i\,d\tau - \oint_{S_\infty}x_i\mathbf P\cdot d\mathbf a = \int P_i\,d\tau$. So $\mathbf p_b = \int\mathbf r\rho_b\,d\tau = \int\mathbf P\,d\tau$. ∎

(c) Uniform $\mathbf P$: $\int\mathbf P\,d\tau = \tfrac43\pi R^3\mathbf P$, the dipole moment in (3.11). (Direct check: $\oint z\,P\cos\theta\,R^2d\Omega = PR^3\cdot2\pi\int\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = \tfrac43\pi R^3P$.)

(d) For $\mathbf P = kr\hat{\mathbf r}$: $\int\mathbf P\,d\tau = 0$ because $\hat{\mathbf r}$ is odd under $\mathbf r\to-\mathbf r$. So $Q = 0$ and $\mathbf p = 0$; all higher multipoles vanish too for a spherically symmetric distribution with $\rho_b$ and $\sigma_b$ depending on $r$ only. The exterior field is therefore zero, as B1(b) found.

(e) With offset $c$, $P_x = f(c + x)$ and $\int P_x\,d\tau = c\int f\,d\tau + \int xf\,d\tau$; the second integral vanishes by oddness and $\int f\,d\tau = 4\pi\int_0^1(1 - r^2)^2r^2dr = 4\pi(\tfrac13 - \tfrac25 + \tfrac17) = 4\pi\cdot\tfrac8{105} = \tfrac{32\pi}{105} = 0.95744$. So $p_x = 0.5\times0.95744 = 0.47872$ for the shipped offset (the grid gives $0.47872$) and $p_x = -0.95744$ for offset $-1.0$ (the grid gives $-0.95744$, $p_y,p_z\sim10^{-18}$). Part 1, "B2" lines.

**B3.** (a) $\rho_b = -r^{-2}\partial_r(r^2\cdot kr^2) = -4kr$, $\sigma_b = kR^2$. $Q_{\text{vol}} = \int_0^R(-4kr)4\pi r^2dr = -16\pi k\,R^4/4 = -4\pi kR^4$; $Q_{\text{surf}} = kR^2\cdot4\pi R^2 = 4\pi kR^4$. Sum zero ✓.

(b) Inside, with $Q_{\text{enc}}(r) = \int_0^r(-4ku)4\pi u^2du = -4\pi kr^4$: $E\cdot4\pi r^2 = -4\pi kr^4/\varepsilon_0$, so $\mathbf E = -kr^2\hat{\mathbf r}/\varepsilon_0 = -\mathbf P/\varepsilon_0$ (again $\mathbf D = 0$). Outside, $Q_b = 0$, so $\mathbf E = 0$.

(c) $V(0) = \int_0^\infty E_r\,dr = \int_0^R\left(-\dfrac{kr^2}{\varepsilon_0}\right)dr = -\dfrac{kR^3}{3\varepsilon_0}$.

(d) From the bound charges, using (3.7)'s kernel $1/\mathscr r = 1/r$ at the centre: $V(0) = \dfrac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\dfrac{\sigma_b\,4\pi R^2}{R} + \int_0^R\dfrac{(-4kr)\,4\pi r^2}{r}dr\right] = \dfrac1{\varepsilon_0}\left[kR^3 - 4k\dfrac{R^3}3\right] = -\dfrac{kR^3}{3\varepsilon_0}$ ✓. (SymPy in Part 1 repeats both for $kr$ and $kr^2$.)

(e) $k = P(R)/R^2 = 10^{-6}/10^{-4} = 10^{-2}$ C/m⁴. $V(0) = -10^{-2}\times10^{-6}/(3\times8.854\times10^{-12}) = -376.47$ V. $E_{\text{in}}(R) = -P(R)/\varepsilon_0 = -1.1294\times10^5$ V/m. $\rho_b(R) = -4kR = -4\times10^{-4}$ C/m³. Why $E = 0$ outside: the bound charge is a negative ball $\rho_b$ wrapped in an exactly cancelling positive spherical shell $\sigma_b$; by Gauss's law a spherically symmetric distribution with zero net charge has no exterior field. The interior field is not zero, and $V(0)\ne0$, because inside the shell the negative charge is unscreened. Part 1, "B3" line.

**B4.** (a) $\mathbf D$ from (3.15) with free charge $q$ at the centre: $\mathbf D = \dfrac q{4\pi r^2}\hat{\mathbf r}$ for all $r>0$, whatever the dielectric. $\mathbf E = \mathbf D/\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r$ inside: $\mathbf E = \dfrac{kq}{\varepsilon_rr^2}\hat{\mathbf r}$ for $r<R$ and $\dfrac{kq}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf r}$ for $r>R$. $\mathbf P = \mathbf D - \varepsilon_0\mathbf E = \left(1 - \dfrac1{\varepsilon_r}\right)\mathbf D = \dfrac{\chi_e}{\varepsilon_r}\dfrac q{4\pi r^2}\hat{\mathbf r}$ for $r<R$, $0$ outside.

(b) Surface: $\sigma_b(R) = P(R) = \dfrac{\chi_e}{\varepsilon_r}\dfrac q{4\pi R^2} = \dfrac23\times\dfrac{10^{-9}}{4\pi\times0.0025} = 2.122\times10^{-8}$ C/m²; total $+\tfrac23q = +0.6667$ nC. Centre: for $r>0$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf P = 0$; at the origin $\nabla\cdot(\hat{\mathbf r}/r^2) = 4\pi\delta^3(\mathbf r)$ (EM-1), so $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf P = -\dfrac{\chi_e}{\varepsilon_r}\dfrac q{4\pi}4\pi\delta^3 = -\dfrac{\chi_e}{\varepsilon_r}q\,\delta^3(\mathbf r)$: a bound point charge $-\tfrac23q = -0.6667$ nC sitting on the free one. Total bound charge $-0.6667 + 0.6667 = 0$ ✓. Net charge at the centre: $q - \tfrac23q = \tfrac13q = q/\varepsilon_r$, which gives $E = kq/(\varepsilon_rr^2)$ directly by Gauss's law for $\mathbf E$.

(c) $E(2\text{ cm}) = 8.988\times10^9\times10^{-9}/(3\times4\times10^{-4}) = 7490$ V/m. At $R$: $E_{\text{in}} = 8.988/(3\times0.0025) = 1198.3$ V/m, $E_{\text{out}} = 8.988/0.0025 = 3595.0$ V/m (equal to the field with no dielectric at all, since the total charge inside is $q$). Jump $= 3595.0 - 1198.3 = 2396.7$ V/m $= \sigma_b/\varepsilon_0 = 2.122\times10^{-8}/8.854\times10^{-12} = 2397$ V/m ✓ (3.16).

(d) (3.19) with $\rho_f = q\delta^3$: $\rho_b = -\dfrac{\chi_e}{1 + \chi_e}q\delta^3 = -\dfrac{\chi_e}{\varepsilon_r}q\delta^3$ ✓, the same. The sphere's finite size appears only as the compensating $+\tfrac23q$ on the surface. Part 7, "B4" line.

**B5.** (a) Planar symmetry and no free charge between the plates: $D$ is uniform and equals $\sigma_f$. $V = E_1d_1 + E_2d_2 = \dfrac D{\varepsilon_0}\left(\dfrac{d_1}{\varepsilon_1} + \dfrac{d_2}{\varepsilon_2}\right)$, so $D = \dfrac{\varepsilon_0V}{d_1/\varepsilon_1 + d_2/\varepsilon_2} = \dfrac{8.854\times10^{-12}\times200}{0.5\times10^{-3} + 0.4\times10^{-3}} = 1.9676\times10^{-6}$ C/m². $E_1 = D/2\varepsilon_0 = 1.1111\times10^5$ V/m, $E_2 = D/5\varepsilon_0 = 4.4444\times10^4$ V/m. Check: $111.11 + 88.89 = 200$ V ✓.

(b) $Q = DA = 1.9676\times10^{-6}\times2\times10^{-3} = 3.935$ nC, $C = Q/V = 19.68$ pF. Check by series: $C_1 = 2\varepsilon_0A/1\text{ mm} = 35.42$ pF, $C_2 = 5\varepsilon_0A/2\text{ mm} = 44.27$ pF, $C = C_1C_2/(C_1 + C_2) = 19.68$ pF ✓.

(c) $P_i = D(1 - 1/\varepsilon_i)$: $P_1 = 9.838\times10^{-7}$, $P_2 = 1.5741\times10^{-6}$ C/m², both along $\mathbf E$ (from the positive to the negative plate). With $y$ from the positive plate, $\sigma_b = \mathbf P\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}$: layer 1 at the positive plate ($\hat{\mathbf n} = -\hat{\mathbf y}$) $-P_1 = -9.838\times10^{-7}$; layer 1 at the interface ($+\hat{\mathbf y}$) $+P_1 = +9.838\times10^{-7}$; layer 2 at the interface ($-\hat{\mathbf y}$) $-P_2 = -1.5741\times10^{-6}$; layer 2 at the negative plate $+P_2 = +1.5741\times10^{-6}$ C/m². Sum: $0$ ✓.

(d) At the interface, adding the two faces: $\sigma_b = P_1 - P_2 = (\mathbf P_1 - \mathbf P_2)\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}_{12} = -5.903\times10^{-7}$ C/m² (the formula after Corollary 3.4). Gauss with all charge, pillbox on the interface (no free charge): $\varepsilon_0(E_2 - E_1) = 8.854\times10^{-12}(4.4444 - 11.111)\times10^4 = -5.903\times10^{-7}$ ✓. Cause: $\rho_b = 0$ in each layer by (3.19), but $\mathbf P$ jumps from $0.50D$ to $0.80D$ across the interface; the dipole chains on the right are stronger than on the left, so the exposed negative ends on the right outweigh the positive ends on the left. Bound charge appears wherever $\varepsilon_r$ changes along $\mathbf D$. Part 7, "B5" line.

**B6.** Per unit area, slab (thickness $d/2$) and gap (thickness $d/2$) both carry $D$: $E_s = D/\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r$, $E_g = D/\varepsilon_0$, $P = D(1 - 1/\varepsilon_r)$ in the slab.
(a) $W = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac d2(DE_s + DE_g) = \dfrac{D^2d}{4\varepsilon_0}\left(\dfrac1{\varepsilon_r} + 1\right) = \dfrac{D^2d}{\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{\varepsilon_r + 1}{4\varepsilon_r} = \dfrac5{16} = 0.3125$ (units $D^2d/\varepsilon_0$, $\varepsilon_r = 4$). $\dfrac{\varepsilon_0}2\int E^2 = \dfrac{\varepsilon_0}2\cdot\dfrac d2(E_s^2 + E_g^2) = \dfrac{D^2d}{4\varepsilon_0}\left(\dfrac1{\varepsilon_r^2} + 1\right) = \dfrac14\cdot\dfrac{17}{16} = \dfrac{17}{64} = 0.265625$. $\tfrac12\int\mathbf P\cdot\mathbf E = \tfrac12\cdot\dfrac d2PE_s = \dfrac{D^2d}{4\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r^2} = \dfrac3{64} = 0.046875$.
(b) $\dfrac{\frac12\int\mathbf P\cdot\mathbf E}W = \dfrac{(\varepsilon_r - 1)/4\varepsilon_r^2}{(\varepsilon_r + 1)/4\varepsilon_r} = \dfrac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r(\varepsilon_r + 1)} = \dfrac3{20} = 0.15$. Check of (3.24): $\frac{17}{64} + \frac3{64} = \frac{20}{64} = \frac5{16}$ ✓.
(c) $V = E_sd/2 + E_gd/2$, so $\tfrac12DV = \dfrac{D^2d}{4\varepsilon_0}\left(\dfrac1{\varepsilon_r} + 1\right) = W$. It is $W = \tfrac12\int\mathbf D\cdot\mathbf E$ that equals $\tfrac12QV$; the $\tfrac{\varepsilon_0}2\int E^2$ is smaller by the spring energy.
(d) A molecule with $\mathbf p = \alpha\mathbf E$ has stretched its internal spring to store $\tfrac12\alpha E^2 = \tfrac12\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E$ (the work $\int_0^E\alpha E'\,dE'$); $N$ per volume gives $\tfrac12\mathbf P\cdot\mathbf E$. $W$ includes this energy; $\tfrac{\varepsilon_0}2\int E^2$ is the energy of assembling the final charges, bound ones placed by hand. The project's grid gives the fraction as $0.156$ rather than $0.150$ only because the single interface face carries the harmonic-mean $\varepsilon$ with the mean $E$, which is exact for $\mathbf D\cdot\mathbf E$ but not for $E^2$; the analytic numbers above are asserted in Part 3 ("B6" lines).

**B7.** (a) A uniform cylinder of charge density $\rho$, radius $R$, long: Gauss with a coaxial cylinder of radius $s<R$ and length $L$: $E\,2\pi sL = \rho\pi s^2L/\varepsilon_0$, so $\mathbf E = \rho\mathbf s/2\varepsilon_0$ ($\mathbf s$ the vector from the axis). Two cylinders, $+\rho$ at $+\mathbf d/2$ and $-\rho$ at $-\mathbf d/2$ ($\mathbf d = d\hat{\mathbf x}$), overlap where $\mathbf E = \dfrac\rho{2\varepsilon_0}\left[(\mathbf s - \tfrac{\mathbf d}2) - (\mathbf s + \tfrac{\mathbf d}2)\right] = -\dfrac{\rho\mathbf d}{2\varepsilon_0} = -\dfrac{\mathbf P}{2\varepsilon_0}$ with $\mathbf P = \rho\mathbf d$. The sphere's $\tfrac13$ and the cylinder's $\tfrac12$ are $1/n$ with $n = \nabla\cdot\mathbf r$ for the dimension of the cross-section ($3$ and $2$); the slab has $n = 1$ and gives $P/\varepsilon_0$.

(b) $V_{\text{in}} = -As\cos\phi$, $V_{\text{out}} = -E_0s\cos\phi + B\cos\phi/s$. Why only $n = 1$: the field at infinity is a pure $\cos\phi$ harmonic. For every other $n$ the two matching conditions on $(a_n, b_n)$ are homogeneous with determinant proportional to $\varepsilon_{\text{in}} + \varepsilon_{\text{out}}\ne0$, so $a_n = b_n = 0$; the $\ln s$ and constant terms of EM-3 (3.24) vanish because $V$ is odd under $x\to-x$. Matching at $s = R$:
- $V$ continuous: $-AR = -E_0R + B/R$, so $B = R^2(E_0 - A)$.
- $D^\perp$ continuous ($\varepsilon\,\partial_sV$, no free charge): $-\varepsilon_{\text{in}}A = \varepsilon_{\text{out}}(-E_0 - B/R^2)$, i.e. $\varepsilon_{\text{in}}A = \varepsilon_{\text{out}}(E_0 + B/R^2) = \varepsilon_{\text{out}}(2E_0 - A)$.
Hence $A = \dfrac{2\varepsilon_{\text{out}}}{\varepsilon_{\text{in}} + \varepsilon_{\text{out}}}E_0$, $B = R^2E_0\dfrac{\varepsilon_{\text{in}} - \varepsilon_{\text{out}}}{\varepsilon_{\text{in}} + \varepsilon_{\text{out}}}$, and $\mathbf E_{\text{in}} = A\hat{\mathbf x}$ ✓. (SymPy in Part 4, for general $\varepsilon_{\text{in}}$, $\varepsilon_{\text{out}}$.) The trap is the second condition: continuity of $\partial_sV$ alone gives $A = E_0 + B/R^2$ and the wrong answer $A = E_0$.

(c) Guess uniform $\mathbf P$: $E_{\text{in}} = E_0 - P/2\varepsilon_0$ by (a), and $P = \varepsilon_0\chi_eE_{\text{in}}$, so $E_{\text{in}}(1 + \chi_e/2) = E_0$, $E_{\text{in}} = \dfrac{2E_0}{2 + \chi_e} = \dfrac{2E_0}{\varepsilon_r + 1}$ ✓, the (b) result with $\varepsilon_{\text{out}} = \varepsilon_0$. For $\varepsilon_r = 4$: $0.4E_0$.

(ii) The difference between the two boundary conditions isolates the box truncation, and times $L^2$ it is constant to $3\%$ ($0.215, 0.210, 0.209$, approaching a limit as the $L^{-4}$ correction dies). Reason: uniform data omit the term $B x/s^2$ on the boundary; relative to the retained $E_0x$ it is of size $B/L^2$, and the interior responds with a geometric factor of the square box, here $0.209/0.6 = 0.35$. Prediction for $L = 32R$: $0.209/32^2 = 2.0\times10^{-4}$, i.e. $0.05\%$ of $0.4$, negligible against the staircase error. **C3.** (a) $\langle\mathbf E\rangle = \dfrac1{\mathcal V}\int_{\text{ball}}\mathbf E\,d\tau = -\dfrac1{\mathcal V}\int\nabla V\,d\tau$. Apply the divergence theorem to $V\mathbf c$ with $\mathbf c$ any constant vector: $\nabla\cdot(V\mathbf c) = \mathbf c\cdot\nabla V$, so $\int_{\mathcal V}\nabla V\,d\tau = \oint V\hat{\mathbf n}\,da$. If the point charge $q$ is inside, cut a small ball of radius $\epsilon$ around it: $V = q/4\pi\varepsilon_0\epsilon$ there, area $4\pi\epsilon^2$, so the small sphere contributes at most $q\epsilon/\varepsilon_0\to0$. Hence $\langle\mathbf E\rangle = -\dfrac1{\mathcal V}\oint V\hat{\mathbf n}\,da$ with $\mathcal V = \tfrac43\pi R_s^3$ and $\hat{\mathbf n} = \hat{\mathbf r}$ on the sphere of radius $R_s$.

(b) Take $z$ along $\mathbf r'$ and $\gamma$ the angle between $\mathbf r$ and $\mathbf r'$. For $r' < R_s$ on the sphere, EM-3 (3.27): $\dfrac1{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r'|} = \sum_\ell\dfrac{r'^\ell}{R_s^{\ell+1}}P_\ell(\cos\gamma)$. The $x,y$ components of $\oint V\hat{\mathbf r}\,da$ vanish by symmetry about the $z$ axis. The $z$ component uses $\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\hat{\mathbf z} = \cos\gamma = P_1$ and orthogonality $\int_{-1}^1P_\ell P_1\,du = \tfrac23\delta_{\ell1}$:
$$\oint V\cos\gamma\,da = R_s^2\,2\pi\frac q{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{r'}{R_s^2}\cdot\frac23 = \frac q{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{4\pi r'}3.$$
So $\langle\mathbf E\rangle = -\dfrac3{4\pi R_s^3}\cdot\dfrac q{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\dfrac{4\pi r'}3\hat{\mathbf z} = -\dfrac{q\,\mathbf r'}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_s^3}$, which is (3.13) for one charge. ∎

(c) For $r' > R_s$: $\dfrac1{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r'|} = \sum_\ell\dfrac{R_s^\ell}{r'^{\ell+1}}P_\ell(\cos\gamma)$, and $\oint V\cos\gamma\,da = R_s^2\,2\pi\dfrac q{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{R_s}{r'^2}\cdot\dfrac23 = \dfrac q{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{4\pi R_s^3}{3r'^2}$, so $\langle\mathbf E\rangle = -\dfrac q{4\pi\varepsilon_0r'^2}\hat{\mathbf z}$. This is the field of $q$ at the origin: it points away from $q$, i.e. along $-\hat{\mathbf z}$ for $q>0$ at $r'\hat{\mathbf z}$ ✓. That is Lemma 3.6(a).

(d) The only angular dependence that $\hat{\mathbf n}$ brings to the integral is $\ell = 1$ (its component along $\mathbf r'$ is $P_1(\cos\gamma)$, its perpendicular components are $\ell = 1$ harmonics that vanish on integrating around the axis). Orthogonality of the Legendre polynomials then kills every other term of the expansion of $V$: only the dipole moment of the charge about the centre, $q\mathbf r'$, can contribute to the average. This is why (3.13) depends only on $\mathbf p$ and not on the arrangement inside the ball. (Part 2 checks both cases by Gauss–Legendre quadrature on the sphere, to $10^{-8}$.)
