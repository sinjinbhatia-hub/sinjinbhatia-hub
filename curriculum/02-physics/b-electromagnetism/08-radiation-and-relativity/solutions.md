## A. Warm-up

**A1.** (a) $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.995^2} = 10.01$. In the Earth frame the muon's clock runs slow, so its mean lifetime is $\gamma\tau = 22.00\ \mu$s and its mean range $\gamma\beta c\tau = 6.562$ km. Without time dilation it would be $\beta c\tau = 655.4$ m, and essentially none would survive 10 km ($e^{-10/0.655}\approx2\times10^{-7}$).

(b) In the muon's frame the muon lives $\tau$ and the atmosphere rushes past at $\beta c$, contracted to $10/\gamma = 0.9987$ km. The surviving fraction is $e^{-0.9987/0.6554} = 0.2178$ in the muon frame and $e^{-10/6.562} = 0.2178$ in the Earth frame. One frame says "slow clock," the other "short atmosphere"; the number of muons reaching the ground is a frame-independent count, so it must agree.

**A2.** $E = K + mc^2 = 1000 + 938.27 = 1938.27$ MeV, so $\gamma = E/(mc^2) = 2.0658$ and $\beta = \sqrt{1 - 1/\gamma^2} = 0.8750$. From (3.25), $pc = \sqrt{E^2 - m^2c^4} = \sqrt{1938.27^2 - 938.27^2} = 1696.0$ MeV. Check: $\gamma\beta mc^2 = 2.0658\times0.8750\times938.27 = 1696.0$ MeV, consistent with $\mathbf p = \gamma m\mathbf v$.

**A3.** $(700/450)^4 = 5.855$. Fact 1: the molecular electrons are driven far below their ultraviolet resonance, so the induced dipole amplitude $p_0$ is the same at both wavelengths (EM-7 §3.13). Fact 2: by (3.16) the radiated power at fixed $p_0$ goes as $\omega^4\propto\lambda^{-4}$.

**A4.** (a) $a = evB/m_e = (1.602\times10^{-19})(10^6)(1.0)/(9.109\times10^{-31}) = 1.759\times10^{17}$ m/s². Larmor (3.19): $P = \mu_0e^2a^2/(6\pi c) = 1.766\times10^{-19}$ W.

(b) The magnetic force does no work, so the only energy change is radiation: $dK/dt = -P$. Since $a = evB/m_e$, $P = \frac{\mu_0e^4B^2}{6\pi cm_e^2}v^2 = \frac{\mu_0e^4B^2}{3\pi cm_e^3}K$, so $K\propto e^{-t/T}$ with
$$T = \frac{3\pi cm_e^3}{\mu_0e^4B^2} = \frac{3\pi\varepsilon_0m_e^3c^3}{e^4B^2} = 2.579\ \text{s}.$$
Check: $T = K/P = 4.555\times10^{-19}/1.766\times10^{-19} = 2.579$ s. The cyclotron period is $2\pi m_e/(eB) = 3.6\times10^{-11}$ s, so the electron makes $\sim10^{11}$ turns per e-folding; in a laboratory collisions matter far sooner. Radiation loss matters only for relativistic electrons ($P\propto\gamma^4$) or very strong fields.

**A5.** The boost is along $x$; $B_z$ is transverse. By (3.26) with $\mathbf E = 0$: $\bar E_y = \gamma(E_y - vB_z) = -\gamma vB = -1.000\times10^3$ V/m, $\bar E_x = \bar E_z = 0$; $\bar B_z = \gamma(B_z - vE_y/c^2) = \gamma B$, with $\gamma - 1 = 5.6\times10^{-12}$, so $\bar B_z = 1.000$ T. Invariants: $\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ and $\bar{\mathbf E}\cdot\bar{\mathbf B} = 0$ (perpendicular). $E^2 - c^2B^2 = -c^2(1\ \text{T})^2 = -8.988\times10^{16}$ V²/m²; in $\bar S$, $\gamma^2v^2B^2 - c^2\gamma^2B^2 = -\gamma^2B^2(c^2 - v^2) = -c^2B^2$, the same. A conductor moving at $\mathbf v$ in $\mathbf B$ has free charges feeling $q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B = -qvB\hat{\mathbf y}$ in the lab; in its own frame they feel the electric force $q\bar{\mathbf E}$, the same vector. EM-6's motional EMF and the "electric field in the moving frame" are one effect.

**A6.** (a) Lowering an index with $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ multiplies by $-1$ each time that index is 0. $F_{0i} = (-1)(+1)F^{0i} = -E_i/c$, $F_{i0} = E_i/c$, $F_{ij} = F^{ij}$:
$$F_{\mu\nu} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -E_x/c & -E_y/c & -E_z/c\\ E_x/c & 0 & B_z & -B_y\\ E_y/c & -B_z & 0 & B_x\\ E_z/c & B_y & -B_x & 0\end{pmatrix}.$$
(b) $F^\mu{}_\nu = F^{\mu\alpha}\eta_{\alpha\nu}$ flips the sign of column 0 only: $F^0{}_i = E_i/c$ and $F^i{}_0 = -F^{i0} = E_i/c$. So $F^0{}_1 = F^1{}_0 = E_x/c$: the mixed tensor is symmetric in its $0i$ block, not antisymmetric. Antisymmetry is a property of $F^{\mu\nu}$ or $F_{\mu\nu}$, not of the matrix of the mixed form.
(c) $F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} = \sum_{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$. Terms $(0, i)$ and $(i, 0)$: each is $(-E_i/c)(E_i/c)$ or $(E_i/c)(-E_i/c)$, total $-2E^2/c^2$. Terms $(i, j)$, $i\ne j$: $(F^{ij})^2$, and each $B_k$ appears in two entries ($F^{12} = B_z$, $F^{21} = -B_z$), total $2B^2$. Sum: $2(B^2 - E^2/c^2)$. The project checks this and $F_{\mu\nu}G^{\mu\nu} = -4\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B/c$ for random fields.

## B. Core

**B1.** (a) The wire is neutral, so $V = 0$. By (3.2), $\mathbf A = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\hat{\mathbf z}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{I(t - \mathscr r/c)}{\mathscr r}dz$ with $\mathscr r = \sqrt{s^2 + z^2}$. The integrand is nonzero only where $t - \mathscr r/c\ge0$, that is $\sqrt{s^2 + z^2}\le ct$, which requires $t\ge s/c$ and then $|z|\le z_0 = \sqrt{c^2t^2 - s^2}$. For $t < s/c$, $\mathbf A = 0$: the news has not arrived. For $t > s/c$, using $\int dz/\sqrt{s^2 + z^2} = \ln(z + \sqrt{s^2 + z^2})$:
$$A_z = \frac{\mu_0I_0}{4\pi}\,2\int_0^{z_0}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{s^2 + z^2}} = \frac{\mu_0I_0}{2\pi}\ln\frac{z_0 + \sqrt{s^2 + z_0^2}}{s} = \frac{\mu_0I_0}{2\pi}\ln\frac{\sqrt{c^2t^2 - s^2} + ct}{s},$$
since $\sqrt{s^2 + z_0^2} = ct$.

(b) $\mathbf E = -\partial_t\mathbf A$. With $\Sigma = \sqrt{c^2t^2 - s^2}$, $\partial_t\Sigma = c^2t/\Sigma$ and $\partial_t\ln(ct + \Sigma) = \frac{c + c^2t/\Sigma}{ct + \Sigma} = \frac{c(\Sigma + ct)/\Sigma}{ct + \Sigma} = \frac{c}{\Sigma}$. So
$$\mathbf E = -\frac{\mu_0I_0c}{2\pi\sqrt{c^2t^2 - s^2}}\hat{\mathbf z}.$$
$\mathbf B = \nabla\times(A_z\hat{\mathbf z}) = -\partial_sA_z\,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$. $\partial_s\Sigma = -s/\Sigma$, so $\partial_s[\ln(ct + \Sigma) - \ln s] = \frac{-s/\Sigma}{ct + \Sigma} - \frac1s = -\frac{s^2 + \Sigma(ct + \Sigma)}{s\Sigma(ct + \Sigma)}$. The numerator is $s^2 + ct\Sigma + c^2t^2 - s^2 = ct(\Sigma + ct)$, so $\partial_s[\cdots] = -\frac{ct}{s\Sigma}$ and
$$\mathbf B = \frac{\mu_0I_0}{2\pi s}\frac{ct}{\sqrt{c^2t^2 - s^2}}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}.$$
(c) As $t\to\infty$, $ct/\Sigma\to1$ and $1/\Sigma\to0$: $\mathbf B\to\frac{\mu_0I_0}{2\pi s}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$ (EM-5's wire) and $\mathbf E\to0$. The transient $\mathbf E$ points along $-\hat{\mathbf z}$, opposing the current: it is the induced (Lenz) field of the growing $\mathbf A$, the back-EMF that a real circuit would have to push against. Both fields blow up at the front $t = s/c$ because the switch-on was instantaneous; a finite rise time smooths them.

**B2.** (a) Differentiate $c(t - t_r) = \mathscr r$ with respect to $t$ at fixed $\mathbf r$. The right side depends on $t$ only through $t_r$: $\mathscr r^2 = (\mathbf r - \mathbf w(t_r))\cdot(\mathbf r - \mathbf w(t_r))$ gives $2\mathscr r\,\partial_t\mathscr r = -2\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}\cdot\mathbf v\,\partial_tt_r$, so $\partial_t\mathscr r = -\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}}\cdot\mathbf v\,\partial_tt_r$. Then $c - c\,\partial_tt_r = -\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}}\cdot\mathbf v\,\partial_tt_r$, so
$$\frac{\partial t_r}{\partial t} = \frac{c}{c - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}}\cdot\mathbf v} = \frac{c\mathscr r}{c\mathscr r - \boldsymbol{\mathscr r}\cdot\mathbf v} = \frac{\mathscr rc}{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}\cdot\mathbf u}.$$
(b) Take $\nabla$ at fixed $t$: $-c\nabla t_r = \nabla\mathscr r$. Component $i$ of $\nabla(\mathscr r_j\mathscr r_j)$ is $2\mathscr r_j(\delta_{ij} - v_j\partial_it_r)$, so $\nabla\mathscr r = \hat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}} - (\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}}\cdot\mathbf v)\nabla t_r$. Hence $-c\nabla t_r = \hat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}} - (\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}}\cdot\mathbf v)\nabla t_r$, i.e. $\nabla t_r(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}}\cdot\mathbf v - c) = \hat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}}$:
$$\nabla t_r = -\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}}}{c - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}}\cdot\mathbf v} = -\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}}{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}\cdot\mathbf u}.$$
(c) For $\mathbf v = 0$: $\mathbf u = c\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}}$, $\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}\cdot\mathbf u = c\mathscr r$, so $\partial_tt_r = 1$ and $\nabla t_r = -\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}}/c$. In Theorem 3.1 the source point $\mathbf r'$ is a fixed integration variable; $\mathscr r = |\mathbf r - \mathbf r'|$ does not depend on $t_r$, so (3.3) is exact there. The motion of the source enters through $\rho(\mathbf r', t_r)$ itself, and for a point charge it reappears as the Jacobian $1/(1 - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}}\cdot\mathbf v/c)$ in Theorem 3.3.
(d) $V = qc/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\,\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}\cdot\mathbf u)$ depends on $t$ only through $t_r$, so $\partial_tV = (dV/dt_r)\,\partial_tt_r$, and $\partial_tt_r = c/(c - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}}\cdot\mathbf v)\ne1$. Treating $\mathscr r$ as changing at the "instantaneous" rate $-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathscr r}}\cdot\mathbf v$ misses this factor; for head-on approach at $\beta = 0.6$ it is $1/(1 - \beta) = 2.5$.

**B3.** From (3.10), $E/E_{\rm Coulomb} = (1 - \beta^2)/(1 - \beta^2\sin^2\theta)^{3/2}$ at equal present distance $R$. $\gamma = 2.294$.
(a) Ahead, $\theta = 0$: $1 - \beta^2 = 0.1900$.
(b) Broadside, $\theta = 90°$: $(1 - \beta^2)/(1 - \beta^2)^{3/2} = 1/\sqrt{1 - \beta^2} = \gamma = 2.294$.
(c) Ratio $\gamma/\gamma^{-2} = \gamma^3 = 12.07$. (The project checks $\gamma^3$ at $\beta = 0.8$ against the numerical solver.)
(d) $|\mathbf v\times\mathbf E| = vE\sin\theta$. Ahead $\sin\theta = 0$ so $\mathbf B = 0$. Broadside both $\sin\theta = 1$ and $E$ is at its maximum, so $|\mathbf B| = vE/c^2$ is largest there; $\mathbf B$ circles the line of motion, as for a current element.

**B4.** (a) $\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}\times\hat{\boldsymbol\theta} = -\hat{\mathbf r}$, so
$$\mathbf S = \frac{1}{\mu_0}E_\phi B_\theta(\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}\times\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}) = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\frac{\mu_0m_0\omega^2}{4\pi c}\frac{\mu_0m_0\omega^2}{4\pi c^2}\frac{\sin^2\theta}{r^2}\cos^2\omega(t - r/c)\,\hat{\mathbf r} = \frac{\mu_0m_0^2\omega^4}{16\pi^2c^3}\frac{\sin^2\theta}{r^2}\cos^2(\cdot)\,\hat{\mathbf r}.$$
Average: $\langle\mathbf S\rangle = \frac{\mu_0m_0^2\omega^4}{32\pi^2c^3}\frac{\sin^2\theta}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf r}$. Integrate over the sphere ($r^2\,d\Omega$, $\int\sin^2\theta\,d\Omega = 8\pi/3$): $\langle P\rangle = \frac{\mu_0m_0^2\omega^4}{32\pi^2c^3}\cdot\frac{8\pi}{3} = \frac{\mu_0m_0^2\omega^4}{12\pi c^3}$, (3.17).
(b) $m_0 = \pi b^2I_0$ and, from Example 4.3, $p_0 = I_0d/\omega = I_0b/\omega$. Then
$$\frac{P_{\rm mag}}{P_{\rm el}} = \frac{m_0^2/c^3}{p_0^2/c} = \frac{\pi^2b^4I_0^2}{c^2I_0^2b^2/\omega^2} = \Big(\frac{\pi b\omega}{c}\Big)^2.$$
For $b = 0.01$ m, $\omega = 2\pi\times10^8$ s⁻¹: $\pi b\omega/c = 0.06584$, ratio $4.335\times10^{-3}$. With $\omega/c = 2\pi/\lambda$ the ratio is $(2\pi^2b/\lambda)^2$: the $(b/\lambda)^2$ suppression.

**B5.** (a) Circular orbit: $m_ev^2/r = e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)$ gives $a = v^2/r = e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0m_er^2)$ and $E = \tfrac12m_ev^2 - \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r} = -\frac{e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0r}$. *Assumption:* the energy lost per orbit is a small fraction of $|E|$, so at each moment the orbit is circular with the current $r$ (checked in (d)). Energy balance $dE/dt = -P$:
$$\frac{e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\dot r = -\frac{\mu_0e^2}{6\pi c}\frac{e^4}{16\pi^2\varepsilon_0^2m_e^2r^4}.$$
Solve and use $\mu_0 = 1/(\varepsilon_0c^2)$: $\dot r = -\frac{8\pi\varepsilon_0}{e^2}\cdot\frac{e^6}{96\pi^3\varepsilon_0^3c^3m_e^2r^2} = -\frac{e^4}{12\pi^2\varepsilon_0^2m_e^2c^3r^2}$. With $r_e^2 = e^4/(16\pi^2\varepsilon_0^2m_e^2c^4)$, this is $\dot r = -\frac{16}{12}\frac{r_e^2c}{r^2} = -\frac43\frac{r_e^2c}{r^2}$. Separate: $3r^2dr = -4r_e^2c\,dt$, so $r^3 = a_0^3 - 4r_e^2ct$.
(b) $t_f = a_0^3/(4r_e^2c) = (5.292\times10^{-11})^3/(4\times(2.818\times10^{-15})^2\times2.998\times10^8) = 1.556\times10^{-11}$ s.
(c) $v = \sqrt{e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0m_er)}$, so $T = 2\pi r/v\propto r^{3/2}$, $T = T_0(r/a_0)^{3/2}$, $T_0 = 2\pi a_0/v_0 = 1.520\times10^{-16}$ s. From (a), $r/a_0 = (1 - t/t_f)^{1/3}$, so $T = T_0(1 - t/t_f)^{1/2}$. The number of revolutions is
$$N = \int_0^{t_f}\frac{dt}{T(t)} = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_0^{t_f}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{1 - t/t_f}} = \frac{t_f}{T_0}\Big[-2\sqrt{1 - t/t_f}\Big]_0^{t_f} = \frac{2t_f}{T_0} = 2.048\times10^5.$$
(d) In the first orbit the electron radiates $PT_0 = 4.669\times10^{-8}\times1.520\times10^{-16}$ J, a fraction $3.26\times10^{-6}$ of $|E| = 13.6$ eV. The spiral is very tight, so treating each orbit as circular is excellent until $r$ is tiny (where $v\to c$ and the nonrelativistic Larmor formula also fails; neither changes $t_f$ appreciably because the last stage takes negligible time).

**B6.** Lab: ions $+\lambda_0$ at rest, electrons $-\lambda_0$ moving at $-v\hat{\mathbf x}$, $q$ at rest at distance $s$. The electrons' frame $\tilde S$ moves at $-v\hat{\mathbf x}$ relative to the lab, so in $\tilde S$ the ions and $q$ both move at $+v\hat{\mathbf x}$.
(a) Electrons are at rest in $\tilde S$, so their density there is their rest density, $-\lambda_0/\gamma$ (in the lab they were contracted by $\gamma$). Ions have rest density $\lambda_0$ and move at $v$: $\gamma\lambda_0$. Net $\tilde\lambda = \lambda_0(\gamma - 1/\gamma) = \lambda_0\gamma\beta^2 > 0$. The electric force on $q$ is $F_E = \frac{q\lambda_0\gamma\beta^2}{2\pi\varepsilon_0s}$, outward (repulsive for $q > 0$).
(b) The only current in $\tilde S$ is the ions': $\tilde I = \gamma\lambda_0v$ along $+\hat{\mathbf x}$, so $\tilde B = \mu_0\gamma\lambda_0v/(2\pi s)$, circling the wire. $q$ moves at $v$ parallel to $\tilde I$; $q\mathbf v\times\tilde{\mathbf B}$ points toward the wire with magnitude $F_B = qv\tilde B = \frac{\mu_0q\gamma\lambda_0v^2}{2\pi s} = \frac{q\lambda_0\gamma\beta^2}{2\pi\varepsilon_0s}$.
(c) $F_E = F_B$ exactly, in opposite directions: zero net force in $\tilde S$, agreeing with the lab (a transverse force of zero is zero in every frame). No expansion in $v/c$ was made. The lesson matches Theorem 3.8: which part of the force is called "electric" depends on the frame, and the total transforms consistently.

**B7.** (a) $\mathbf v = \mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2 = \frac{EB}{B^2}(\hat{\mathbf y}\times\hat{\mathbf z}) = \frac EB\hat{\mathbf x}$, with $v < c$ because $E < cB$. Both fields are perpendicular to $\mathbf v$. $\mathbf v\times\mathbf B = \frac EB B(\hat{\mathbf x}\times\hat{\mathbf z}) = -E\hat{\mathbf y}$, so $\bar{\mathbf E} = \gamma(\mathbf E + \mathbf v\times\mathbf B) = 0$. $\mathbf v\times\mathbf E = \frac{E^2}{B}\hat{\mathbf z}$, so $\bar{\mathbf B} = \gamma\big(B - \frac{E^2}{Bc^2}\big)\hat{\mathbf z} = \gamma B(1 - \beta^2)\hat{\mathbf z} = \frac B\gamma\hat{\mathbf z}$, with $\beta = E/(cB)$. Invariant check: $-c^2\bar B^2 = -c^2B^2(1 - \beta^2) = E^2 - c^2B^2$.
(b) In $\bar S$ there is only a uniform $\bar{\mathbf B}$, in which a charge gyrates in circles about a fixed centre (EM-5). Transforming back, that centre moves at $\mathbf v = \mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2$ in the lab, for every charge regardless of sign or mass: the $\mathbf E\times\mathbf B$ drift.
(c) $v = E/B = 1.0\times10^3$ m/s along $\hat{\mathbf x}$ (project `[B7]`, which also confirms $\bar{\mathbf E} = 0$). If $E > cB$, the required $v = E/B$ exceeds $c$. Equivalently, $E^2 - c^2B^2 > 0$ is invariant, but a pure magnetic field would make it $-c^2\bar B^2\le0$. (Instead there is then a frame with pure $\mathbf E$.)

**B8.** (a) $\mu = 2$: $\partial_0F^{20} + \partial_1F^{21} + \partial_3F^{23} = -\frac{1}{c^2}\partial_tE_y - \partial_xB_z + \partial_zB_x = \mu_0J_y$, using $F^{20} = -E_y/c$, $F^{21} = -B_z$, $F^{23} = B_x$. Since $(\nabla\times\mathbf B)_y = \partial_zB_x - \partial_xB_z$, this is the $y$ component of Ampère–Maxwell.
(b) From (3.27), $G^{30} = -B_z$, $G^{31} = -E_y/c$, $G^{32} = E_x/c$: $-\frac1c\partial_tB_z - \frac1c\partial_xE_y + \frac1c\partial_yE_x = -\frac1c(\partial_t\mathbf B + \nabla\times\mathbf E)_z = 0$, the $z$ component of Faraday's law.
(c) $S = \partial_\mu\partial_\nu F^{\mu\nu}$. Rename the dummy indices $\mu\leftrightarrow\nu$: $S = \partial_\nu\partial_\mu F^{\nu\mu}$. Partial derivatives commute and $F^{\nu\mu} = -F^{\mu\nu}$, so $S = -\partial_\mu\partial_\nu F^{\mu\nu} = -S$, hence $S = 0$. Applied to (3.30): $\mu_0\partial_\mu J^\mu = 0$, i.e. $\partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot\mathbf J = 0$. Maxwell's equations are inconsistent unless charge is conserved.
(d) Substitute: $\partial_\lambda(\partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu) + \partial_\mu(\partial_\nu A_\lambda - \partial_\lambda A_\nu) + \partial_\nu(\partial_\lambda A_\mu - \partial_\mu A_\lambda)$. The six terms cancel in pairs: $\partial_\lambda\partial_\mu A_\nu$ with $-\partial_\mu\partial_\lambda A_\nu$, $-\partial_\lambda\partial_\nu A_\mu$ with $\partial_\nu\partial_\lambda A_\mu$, $\partial_\mu\partial_\nu A_\lambda$ with $-\partial_\nu\partial_\mu A_\lambda$. For $(1, 2, 3)$: $F_{23} = B_x$, $F_{31} = B_y$, $F_{12} = B_z$ (spatial indices are unchanged by lowering), so the identity reads $\partial_xB_x + \partial_yB_y + \partial_zB_z = 0$. (Choices with one index 0 give Faraday's law.)

**B9.** (a) Uniform circular motion has constant $a = R\omega^2$, so Larmor gives $P = \mu_0q^2R^2\omega^4/(6\pi c)$. A linear oscillation $x = R\cos\omega t$ has $\langle a^2\rangle = R^2\omega^4/2$ and half that power. Ratio 2.
(b) $\mathbf p = qR(\cos\omega t, \sin\omega t, 0)$ is two perpendicular dipoles a quarter period apart. The radiated intensity in direction $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is $\propto|\hat{\mathbf n}\times\ddot{\mathbf p}|^2 = \ddot p^2 - (\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\ddot{\mathbf p})^2$. Averaged over a period the cross term $\langle\ddot p_x\ddot p_y\rangle$ vanishes, $\langle\ddot p_x^2\rangle = \langle\ddot p_y^2\rangle = q^2R^2\omega^4/2$, so $\langle|\hat{\mathbf n}\times\ddot{\mathbf p}|^2\rangle = \frac{q^2R^2\omega^4}{2}(2 - n_x^2 - n_y^2) = \frac{q^2R^2\omega^4}{2}(1 + \cos^2\theta)$. Normalized to the pole, the pattern is $(1 + \cos^2\theta)/2$: maximal along the rotation axis, half as strong in the orbit plane. The reference code in Part 7 builds the trajectory `circ`, runs the same flux integral as Part 3 on a sphere of radius $5\lambda$ ($q = 1$ nC, $\lambda = 1$ m), and prints $P_{\rm num} = 1.7729\times10^{-3}$ W against $1.7728\times10^{-3}$ W from Larmor, ratio to the linear dipole $2.0001$, with the $(1 + \cos^2\theta)/2$ pattern asserted to 2%.

## C. Challenge

**C1.** (a) $\tau = \mu_0e^2/(6\pi m_ec) = 6.266\times10^{-24}$ s. Since $r_e = e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0m_ec^2) = \mu_0e^2/(4\pi m_e)$, $c\tau = \mu_0e^2/(6\pi m_e) = \tfrac23r_e = 1.879\times10^{-15}$ m.
(b) The equation $a = F/m + \tau\dot a$ is first order in $a$. For $t > 0$: $a = F_0/m + Ce^{t/\tau}$; no runaway forces $C = 0$, so $a = F_0/m$. For $t < 0$ ($F = 0$): $a = Ae^{t/\tau}$. $a$ must be continuous at $t = 0$: a jump would make $\dot a$ contain a delta function, and the equation $m\tau\dot a = ma - F$ has no delta function on the right (a step force is finite). So $A = F_0/m$:
$$a(t) = \frac{F_0}{m}\begin{cases}e^{t/\tau}, & t < 0,\\ 1, & t\ge0.\end{cases}$$
Project Your-turn 3 integrates the $t < 0$ equation backward from $a(0) = F_0/m$ and finds $a(-\tau) = 0.3679F_0/m = e^{-1}F_0/m$.
(c) The charge starts accelerating before the force is applied, which violates causality. The violation lasts a few $\tau\sim10^{-23}$ s, over which light travels $\sim10^{-15}$ m; at those scales quantum electrodynamics replaces the classical theory, so no measurement can see the classical prediction.

**C2.** (a) In the rest frame $\bar S$ the photon has $\bar p^\mu = \frac{\bar E}{c}(1, \cos\bar\theta, \sin\bar\theta, 0)$. $\bar S$ moves at $+\beta\hat{\mathbf x}$ relative to the lab, so the lab components follow from the inverse of (3.23): $p^0 = \gamma(\bar p^0 + \beta\bar p^1)$, $p^1 = \gamma(\bar p^1 + \beta\bar p^0)$, $p^2 = \bar p^2$. A photon's direction cosines are $p^i/p^0$ (because $|\mathbf p| = p^0$ for light). So
$$\cos\theta = \frac{\cos\bar\theta + \beta}{1 + \beta\cos\bar\theta},\qquad\sin\theta = \frac{\sin\bar\theta}{\gamma(1 + \beta\cos\bar\theta)}.$$
(b) At $\bar\theta = 90°$: $\cos\theta = \beta$, $\sin\theta = 1/\gamma$. Everything emitted into the forward hemisphere of $\bar S$ ($\bar\theta < 90°$) lands in $\theta < \arcsin(1/\gamma)$, because $\cos\theta$ increases monotonically with $\cos\bar\theta$. For $\gamma = 10$, $\theta = 5.739°$; for $\gamma = 1000$, $\theta = 0.0573°$. (The rest-frame emission is a dipole pattern rather than isotropic, but it is symmetric front to back, so half of it is in the forward hemisphere either way.)
(c) A circulating relativistic charge, in its instantaneous rest frame, radiates like a dipole. Aberration crushes half of that into a cone of angle $1/\gamma$ about the velocity, and the Doppler factor $(1 - \beta\cos\theta)^{-1}$ in Liénard's formula weights the forward direction further. That is the narrowing lobe in `synchrotron.png`, whose full width times $\gamma$ the project prints as roughly constant ($0.67$ at $\beta = 0.9$, $0.63$ at $\beta = 0.99$).

**C3.** In terms of potentials, $\mathcal L = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}|\nabla V + \partial_t\mathbf A|^2 - \frac{1}{2\mu_0}|\nabla\times\mathbf A|^2 - \rho V + \mathbf J\cdot\mathbf A$. The Euler–Lagrange equation for $A_x$ is
$$\partial_t\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_tA_x)} + \sum_j\partial_j\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_jA_x)} = \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial A_x}.$$
1. $\partial\mathcal L/\partial A_x = J_x$.
2. $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_tA_x) = \varepsilon_0(\partial_xV + \partial_tA_x) = -\varepsilon_0E_x$.
3. $B_z = \partial_xA_y - \partial_yA_x$ and $B_y = \partial_zA_x - \partial_xA_z$; $B_x$ does not contain $A_x$. So $\partial(B^2)/\partial(\partial_yA_x) = -2B_z$ and $\partial(B^2)/\partial(\partial_zA_x) = 2B_y$, giving $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_yA_x) = B_z/\mu_0$, $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_zA_x) = -B_y/\mu_0$, and $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_xA_x) = 0$ ($\mathbf E$ depends on $\partial_xV$, not on $\partial_xA_x$).
4. Assemble: $-\varepsilon_0\partial_tE_x + \frac{1}{\mu_0}(\partial_yB_z - \partial_zB_y) = J_x$, that is $(\nabla\times\mathbf B)_x = \mu_0J_x + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_tE_x$, the $x$ component of Ampère–Maxwell. The $y$ and $z$ components follow by the cyclic relabelling $x\to y\to z\to x$, under which $\mathcal L$ is unchanged.
