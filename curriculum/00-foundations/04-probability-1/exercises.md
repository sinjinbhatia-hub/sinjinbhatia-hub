# F4 Exercises

Conventions as in the notes: $q = 1 - p$; $\mathrm{Geom}(p)$ counts *failures* before the first success (support $\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$); $\mathrm{Expo}(\lambda)$ has rate $\lambda$ and mean $1/\lambda$; $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ has the variance second; $\Phi$ is the standard Normal CDF. Every number you compute here is also computed in `project.py` (Part 10 collects the exercise numbers), so you can check yourself.

## A. Warm-up (do all; 5–10 minutes each)

**A1.** (De Méré's two bets, 1654.) (a) You roll one fair die four times. Find the probability of at least one 6. (b) You roll two fair dice 24 times. Find the probability of at least one double-six. (c) De Méré reasoned that since $4/6 = 24/36$, the two bets should be equally favorable, and was puzzled to lose money on the second. Say in one sentence what is wrong with his reasoning. (d) How many rolls of two dice make the double-six bet favorable (probability above $1/2$)?

**A2.** (Two story proofs.) Prove each identity by exhibiting one set that both sides count. No algebra.
(a) $k\binom{n}{k} = n\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ for $1 \le k \le n$.
(b) $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}^2 = \binom{2n}{n}$.
Check (b) numerically for $n = 5$.

**A3.** (Conditional probability from a table.) Two hundred students were tracked through ST-1. The counts:

| | Passed ST-1 | Failed ST-1 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Completed F4 first | 72 | 8 | 80 |
| Skipped F4 | 78 | 42 | 120 |
| Total | 150 | 50 | 200 |

Treat a student drawn uniformly at random from the 200 as the experiment. Let $F$ = completed F4, $S$ = passed. Compute $P(S)$, $P(S \mid F)$, $P(S \mid F^c)$, $P(F \mid S)$, $P(F \mid S^c)$. Verify $P(F \mid S)$ a second way with Bayes' theorem (3.10). Are $F$ and $S$ independent? Give the one-line numerical test.

**A4.** (PMF, CDF, expectation.) Roll two fair dice and let $X$ be the *larger* of the two numbers (the maximum). (a) Find the CDF $F_X(k) = P(X \le k)$ for $k = 1, \dots, 6$ first, by counting; then read off the PMF as the jumps. (b) Compute $\mathbb{E}[X]$, $\mathbb{E}[X^2]$ by LOTUS, and $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ by (3.17). Give exact fractions and decimals.

**A5.** (Normal and Exponential numbers.) (a) $X \sim \mathcal{N}(100, 15^2)$. Find $P(X > 130)$, $P(85 < X < 115)$, and the 99th percentile of $X$. Use $\Phi(2) = 0.97725$ and $\Phi^{-1}(0.99) = 2.3263$. (b) The time $T$ (minutes) until the next request at a server is $\mathrm{Expo}(\lambda)$ with mean 10 minutes. Find $\lambda$, $P(T > 20)$, $P(T < 5)$, $P(T > 30 \mid T > 10)$, the median of $T$, and $\sigma_T$.

## B. Core (do all; these are the module)

**B1.** (Inclusion–exclusion by indicators.) (a) Show $I_{A \cup B \cup C} = 1 - (1 - I_A)(1 - I_B)(1 - I_C)$ as functions on $\Omega$ (check both cases: $\omega$ in the union, $\omega$ not in it). (b) Expand the product, take expectations, and use the fundamental bridge (Theorem 3.9) to prove (3.6). (c) Say in two sentences how the same argument proves the general formula (3.7). (d) Use (3.6) to compute the probability of at least one 6 in three rolls of a die; confirm against $1 - (5/6)^3$.

**B2.** (The two-coin problem.) A bag holds a fair coin and a coin with $P(H) = 3/4$. You draw one uniformly at random and toss it. Let $F$ = the coin is fair, $H_i$ = the $i$-th toss is heads. (a) Compute $P(F \mid H_1)$ and $P(F \mid T_1)$ where $T_1 = H_1^c$. (b) Compute $P(H_2 \mid H_1)$ and compare with $P(H_2)$. Are $H_1$ and $H_2$ independent? Are they conditionally independent given $F$? Prove both answers. (c) Compute $P(F \mid H_1 \cap H_2)$ two ways: directly from the joint probabilities, and by applying Bayes with extra conditioning (3.12) to $H_2$ using the posterior from (a) as the prior. (d) Find a closed form for $P(F \mid H_1 \cap \cdots \cap H_n)$ and evaluate it at $n = 5$. What happens as $n \to \infty$, and why is that the right answer?

**B3.** (Pairwise versus mutual independence.) (a) Toss two fair coins; $A$ = first is heads, $B$ = second is heads, $C$ = the tosses agree. Verify all three pairwise independence conditions and show the triple condition fails. (b) Roll one fair die; $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$, $B = \{3, 4, 5\}$, $C = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$. Show $P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A)P(B)P(C)$ although $A$ and $B$ are *not* independent. Conclude that the triple condition alone is not mutual independence either. (c) Prove: if $A, B, C$ are mutually independent, then $A$ is independent of $B \cap C$ and of $B \cup C$. (d) Show that (c) fails for the pairwise-only events of (a): check $A$ against $B \cap C$.

**B4.** (Geometric variance and the first-success shift.) Let $X \sim \mathrm{Geom}(p)$. (a) The notes found $\operatorname{Var}(X) = q/p^2$ by first-step conditioning. Find it a second way: compute the *factorial moment* $\mathbb{E}[X(X-1)] = \sum_k k(k-1)q^k p$ by differentiating the geometric series twice, then use $\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \mathbb{E}[X(X-1)] + \mathbb{E}[X]$ and (3.17). (b) Let $Y = X + 1$ be the number of *trials* up to and including the first success. Find $\mathbb{E}[Y]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y)$ and say in one sentence why the variance did not change. (c) Show $P(X \ge k) = q^k$ and deduce the discrete memorylessness property $P(X \ge s + t \mid X \ge s) = P(X \ge t)$ for nonnegative integers $s, t$. (d) For $p = 0.2$ give $\mathbb{E}[X]$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)$, $\sigma_X$, $\mathbb{E}[Y]$, and $P(X \ge 10)$.

**B5.** (Hypergeometric by indicators.) Deal 5 cards from a well-shuffled 52-card deck; $X$ = number of aces. (a) Name the distribution with its parameters and write the PMF. (b) Compute $\mathbb{E}[X]$ with indicators. (c) Compute $\operatorname{Cov}(I_1, I_2)$ for the indicators of "card 1 is an ace" and "card 2 is an ace" directly from $\mathbb{E}[I_1 I_2]$, and check it against the formula $-pq/(N-1)$ from §3.10. (d) Compute $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ with the finite-population correction. (e) Compute $P(X \ge 1)$ exactly, and compare both $P(X \ge 1)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ with the $\mathrm{Bin}(5, 1/13)$ approximation. Which quantity does the approximation get exactly right, and why?

**B6.** (Gamma moments.) Let $X \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(a, \lambda)$ with density $\frac{\lambda^a}{\Gamma(a)}x^{a-1}e^{-\lambda x}$ on $x > 0$. (a) Show $\mathbb{E}[X^k] = \dfrac{\Gamma(a + k)}{\Gamma(a)\,\lambda^k}$ for every integer $k \ge 0$ by writing the integral and substituting $u = \lambda x$. (b) Deduce $\mathbb{E}[X] = a/\lambda$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X) = a/\lambda^2$ using Theorem 3.19(a). Check that $a = 1$ reproduces the Exponential. (c) Derive the MGF $M_X(t) = \left(\dfrac{\lambda}{\lambda - t}\right)^a$ for $t < \lambda$. (d) Use Theorems 3.22 and 3.23 to prove the claim stated in §3.13: if $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are i.i.d. $\mathrm{Expo}(\lambda)$ then $X_1 + \cdots + X_n \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(n, \lambda)$. (e) For $\mathrm{Gamma}(3, 2)$ give $\mathbb{E}[X]$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)$, and $\mathbb{E}[X^3]$.

**B7.** (Universality and a Cauchy sampler; extends `project.py`.) The standard Cauchy distribution has PDF $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{\pi(1 + x^2)}$ on $\mathbb{R}$.
(a) Find its CDF $F$ and inverse $F^{-1}$. Write `cauchy_sample(size, rng)` using Theorem 3.15(a), add it to Part 4 of `project.py`, overlay the empirical CDF on $F$, and run `scipy.stats.kstest` against `scipy.stats.cauchy`.
(b) The project's Part 8 shows the running mean of Cauchy samples failing to settle. Prove why: show $\int_{-\infty}^\infty |x| f(x)\,dx = \infty$, so $\mathbb{E}[X]$ does not exist. Then run Part 8 with three different seeds and report the final running means. (Checking "the sample mean is close to 0" is the trap here: close to what?)
(c) Which sample statistics *do* converge for Cauchy data? Compute $F^{-1}(1/2)$ and $F^{-1}(3/4)$, then check the sample median and sample upper quartile of your $10^5$ draws against them.
(d) Give a physical story for the Cauchy: a point source at height 1 above a line emits a ray at an angle $\Theta \sim \mathrm{Unif}(-\pi/2, \pi/2)$ from the vertical. Show the ray hits the line at a Cauchy-distributed position. (Your answer to (a) is this story in disguise.)

**B8.** (The Normal MGF and a sum with zero variance.) (a) Derive $M_Z(t) = e^{t^2/2}$ for $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ by completing the square. Expand $e^{t^2/2}$ as a power series and read off $\mathbb{E}[Z^4]$ and $\mathbb{E}[Z^6]$ by matching coefficients with Theorem 3.20. State the general $\mathbb{E}[Z^{2k}]$. (b) For $X = \mu + \sigma Z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, expand $(\mu + \sigma Z)^k$ and use (a) to find $\mathbb{E}[X^2]$, $\mathbb{E}[X^3]$, $\mathbb{E}[X^4]$ in terms of $\mu, \sigma$. Evaluate for $\mu = 1$, $\sigma = 2$. (c) Toss a fair coin $n = 10$ times; $X$ = number of heads, $Y$ = number of tails. Compute $\operatorname{Var}(X)$, $\operatorname{Var}(Y)$, $\operatorname{Var}(X + Y)$, and from (3.19) deduce $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$. Then compute $\operatorname{Var}(X - Y)$ two ways: from (3.19) with the sign flipped, and by writing $X - Y$ as a function of $X$ alone. Each variance is positive and the variance of the sum is zero; say in one sentence which hypothesis of Theorem 3.14 fails.

## C. Challenge (do at least one)

**C1.** (The Tuesday boy.) A family has two children. Each child is independently a boy or a girl with probability $1/2$, and independently born on each of the seven weekdays with probability $1/7$. You are told: "at least one of the children is a boy born on a Tuesday." (a) Set up a sample space of equally likely outcomes and compute $P(\text{both boys} \mid \text{at least one Tuesday boy})$ exactly. (b) Generalize: if the extra property ("born on a Tuesday") holds for a boy with probability $r$, show the answer is $\dfrac{2 - r}{4 - r}$, and check the limits $r \to 1$ and $r \to 0$ against Trap 3 parts (i) and (ii)/(iii) of §3.7. Explain in words why more specific information pushes the answer toward $1/2$. (c) Confirm (a) by brute-force enumeration in Python (there are $14^2 = 196$ outcomes) and by simulation.

**C2.** (Waiting for $HH$ versus $HT$.) Toss a fair coin repeatedly. Let $W_{HH}$ be the number of tosses until the pattern $HH$ first appears (the toss completing the pattern counts), and $W_{HT}$ likewise for $HT$. (a) By first-step analysis with two states ("no progress" and "last toss was $H$"), show $\mathbb{E}[W_{HT}] = 4$ and $\mathbb{E}[W_{HH}] = 6$. (b) Both patterns have probability $1/4$ on any two given consecutive tosses. Explain in words where the asymmetry comes from. (c) Redo (a) for a coin with $P(H) = p$ and show $\mathbb{E}[W_{HT}] = \dfrac1p + \dfrac1q$ and $\mathbb{E}[W_{HH}] = \dfrac1p + \dfrac{1}{p^2}$. Evaluate both at $p = 0.6$. (d) Simulate both waits ($10^5$ runs each) and compare.

**C3.** (Discrete memorylessness characterizes the Geometric.) Let $X$ take values in $\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ with $0 < P(X = 0) < 1$, and suppose $P(X \ge s + t \mid X \ge s) = P(X \ge t)$ for all nonnegative integers $s, t$. (a) Let $G(k) = P(X \ge k)$. Show $G(s + t) = G(s)G(t)$, deduce $G(k) = G(1)^k$, and conclude $X \sim \mathrm{Geom}(p)$ with $p = P(X = 0)$. Where did you use $0 < P(X = 0) < 1$? (b) Let $T \sim \mathrm{Expo}(\lambda)$ and $X = \lfloor T \rfloor$ (the integer part). Show $X \sim \mathrm{Geom}(p)$ with $p = 1 - e^{-\lambda}$, and explain in one sentence why this had to be true given (a) and Theorem 3.16. (c) For $\lambda = \ln 2$: what are $p$, $\mathbb{E}[X]$, and $P(X \ge 3)$? Check by simulation.

**C4.** (Variance of the number of fixed points.) Let $\sigma$ be a uniformly random permutation of $\{1, \dots, n\}$, $n \ge 2$, and $X = \sum_{j=1}^n I_j$ the number of fixed points, with $I_j$ the indicator of $\sigma(j) = j$. (a) Compute $\mathbb{E}[I_i I_j]$ for $i \ne j$. (b) Expand $X^2 = \sum_j I_j + \sum_{i \ne j} I_i I_j$ and show $\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 2$, hence $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 1$ for every $n \ge 2$. (c) For $n = 4$, list the number of permutations with exactly $k$ fixed points for $k = 0, \dots, 4$ and recompute $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and $\mathbb{E}[X^2]$ from this distribution. (d) The notes claim $X \to \mathrm{Pois}(1)$ in distribution. Using $P(X = k) = \frac{1}{k!}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}\frac{(-1)^i}{i!}$ (Example 4.5), compute $P(X = 0), P(X = 1), P(X = 2)$ for $n = 10$ and compare with $e^{-1}/k!$. Then verify by brute force over all $n!$ permutations for $n = 4, 5, 6$ that $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 1$ exactly (extend `project.py` Part 1 or Part 10).
