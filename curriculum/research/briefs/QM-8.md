§1: almost nothing in QM is exactly solvable (hydrogen and the oscillator nearly exhaust the list), so the subject's working tools are approximations: Schrödinger (1926) and Rayleigh (1894, for acoustics) for perturbation theory; Ritz (1909) for the variational method, which Hylleraas used (1929) to compute helium's ground state to spectroscopic accuracy and prove QM works for more than one electron; Wentzel, Kramers, and Brillouin (1926) for the semiclassical approximation that connects to CM-8; Dirac (1927) for time-dependent perturbation theory and the first quantum theory of radiation; Fermi's "golden rule" (1950, named by Fermi, derived by Dirac); Rabi (1937) for the resonance method behind NMR, atomic clocks, and every quantum gate. The forcing problem: the real hydrogen atom has fine structure, the Zeeman and Stark effects need a field, atoms emit light at a rate — none of these are exact problems, all of them are "almost-solved problem plus a small correction". §2: perturbation theory is Taylor expansion in the strength of the correction, with LA-8's eigenbases doing the bookkeeping; the first-order energy shift is just the expectation value of the perturbation in the unperturbed state; degeneracy means the unperturbed basis is ambiguous and the perturbation picks it; the variational principle is "any trial wavefunction's energy is an upper bound, so minimize"; WKB is "locally, the wavefunction is a plane wave with the local momentum" (CM-8's Hamilton–Jacobi with ℏ); time-dependent perturbation theory is "the perturbation stirs the amplitudes among the unperturbed states"; resonance is when the stirring frequency matches a level spacing.

§8 honest: perturbation theory's bookkeeping (which order, which states, which denominators) is where everyone makes errors — write the λ powers explicitly; degenerate PT's "good states" are the single most confusing idea and the commuting-operator theorem is the cure; the Thomas factor in spin–orbit coupling is stated, not derived (it's special relativity); the WKB connection formulas are genuinely hard and we use them without full proof; the RWA is an approximation that fails for strong driving and students apply it everywhere; Fermi's golden rule needs a continuum and students apply it to two levels; the perturbation series for λx⁴ diverges for every λ ≠ 0 and still gives great answers at low order — asymptotic series are weird and the module shows it.
