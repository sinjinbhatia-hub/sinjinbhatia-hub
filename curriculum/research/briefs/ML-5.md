# Brief: ML-5 — Trees and ensembles: decision trees, bagging, random forests, boosting, gradient boosting
Folder: curriculum/04-machine-learning/05-trees-and-ensembles/ · ~10 hours · prereqs ML-1 (bias–variance, CV), F5 (entropy), ML-2 (gradient descent in function space) · unlocks ML-10 (ensembles vs deep nets), practical tabular ML

Conventions: a tree T partitions the feature space into regions R_m with constant predictions c_m; impurity measures (Gini, entropy, variance); the split (feature j, threshold s); depth, leaves, min samples; bagging with B bootstrap samples; random forest with m features per split; boosting with learning rate ν, M rounds; residuals r_i; the additive model F_M(x) = Σν h_m(x).

§1: Morgan and Sonquist (1963) built the first automatic interaction detector; Breiman, Friedman, Olshen and Stone's CART (1984) made trees a statistical method; Breiman's bagging (1996) and random forests (2001) showed that averaging many unstable models beats one careful model; Freund and Schapire's AdaBoost (1997) came from learning theory (can weak learners be combined into a strong one? yes — and the Gödel Prize); Friedman (2001) reinterpreted boosting as gradient descent in function space; XGBoost (2016) and LightGBM made gradient boosting the default winner on tabular data. The forcing problem: linear models (ML-3) and kernels (ML-4) assume smooth functions of all features; real tabular data has interactions, thresholds, mixed types, and irrelevant features, and trees handle all of that with no preprocessing — but a single tree is a high-variance estimator, and the ensemble methods are the two different cures (averaging to cut variance; sequential fitting to cut bias). §2: a decision tree is twenty questions — each split cuts the space with an axis-aligned plane, and the leaves predict constants; it can fit anything (deep) or nothing (shallow), and its predictions jump when the data wobble (variance); bagging grows many trees on bootstrap resamples (ST-2) and averages them, random forests decorrelate the trees by hiding features, and the average of many noisy nearly-independent predictors has much lower variance (F5: Var of a mean); boosting instead grows trees one at a time, each one fitting what the previous ones got wrong — gradient descent where the "parameter" is the function itself.

§8 honest: greedy splitting is myopic and the XOR example should be internalized before trusting a tree's "importance"; impurity-based importance is biased and everyone uses it anyway; the bagging variance formula is one line and explains everything about random forests — learn it; "random forests don't overfit" is a half-truth; AdaBoost's exponential-loss derivation is the most elegant thing in the module and the most skipped; gradient boosting is gradient descent in function space and that sentence takes a week to mean something; the three regularizers of boosting interact and the defaults are tuned by folklore; trees extrapolate as constants and will embarrass you outside the training range; the tabular-data consensus is empirical and could change.
