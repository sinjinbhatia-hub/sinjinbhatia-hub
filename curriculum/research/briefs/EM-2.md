# Brief: EM-2 — Electrostatics: Coulomb → Gauss → Poisson; potential, energy, conductors
Folder: curriculum/02-physics/b-electromagnetism/02-electrostatics/ · ~12 hours · prereq EM-1 · unlocks EM-3
Pair with: Griffiths ch. 2; Purcell & Morin ch. 1–3; MIT 8.07 notes (Guth) lectures 1–4; Feynman Lectures II ch. 4–8.
Conventions: SI; ε₀; Coulomb's constant 1/4πε₀; E in V/m; potential V (and φ where V would clash); 𝓇 = r − r'; V(∞) = 0 for localized charges.

§1: Coulomb (1785) measured the inverse-square law with a torsion balance; Gauss's law (1835/1867) recast it as a statement about flux; Poisson (1813) and Green (1828) turned it into a boundary-value problem; the problem that forced the field concept: action-at-a-distance was philosophically intolerable and computationally hopeless for continuous charge distributions — Faraday's lines of force and the field as a thing in its own right. §2: field lines as flow lines of an incompressible fluid emanating from positive charges; Gauss's law as "count the lines"; potential as height on a landscape, E as the downhill slope; conductors as surfaces of constant height.

Topics in order: Coulomb's law and superposition; the field E = (1/4πε₀)Σ q_i 𝓇̂_i/𝓇_i² and the continuum version with ρ, σ, λ; FIELD-LINE picture and its rules; GAUSS'S LAW integral form derived from Coulomb via the delta-function identity of EM-1 (∇·E = ρ/ε₀ then the divergence theorem) — and the converse argument that Gauss + spherical symmetry gives back Coulomb; applications with full derivations: uniformly charged sphere (inside/outside), infinite line, infinite plane, spherical shell, coaxial cylinders, and the statement of which symmetries make Gauss useful (spherical, cylindrical, planar — and why nothing else); ∇×E = 0 for electrostatics (derive from the Coulomb field) and hence the POTENTIAL V with E = −∇V (EM-1's theorem); V = (1/4πε₀)∫ρ dτ'/𝓇 (derive); POISSON'S and LAPLACE'S equations ∇²V = −ρ/ε₀; the potential of standard configurations (sphere, shell, line (log; reference point at infinity fails — explain), dipole); boundary conditions at a surface charge: E_above⊥ − E_below⊥ = σ/ε₀, tangential E continuous, V continuous, ∂V/∂n jumps by −σ/ε₀ (derive all from Gauss and Stokes with a pillbox and a loop); WORK AND ENERGY: work to move a charge, W = QΔV; energy of a point-charge configuration W = ½Σq_iV(r_i) (derive the ½); the continuum W = ½∫ρV dτ → W = (ε₀/2)∫E² dτ (derive via Poisson and integration by parts/Green's identity; discuss where the energy "is", and the infinite self-energy of a point charge honestly); energy of a uniformly charged sphere (3Q²/20πε₀R) computed two ways; CONDUCTORS: E = 0 inside, ρ = 0 inside, charge on the surface, V constant, E ⊥ surface (derive each), induced charges, Faraday cage, the surface force per unit area σ²/2ε₀ (derive, with the famous average-field argument), CAPACITORS: C = Q/V, parallel plate, spherical, cylindrical (derive each), energy W = ½CV² = Q²/2C (derive), series/parallel; cavities in conductors and the uniqueness-theorem preview (EM-3); a short section connecting Poisson's equation to the student's PDE knowledge and to heat conduction and gravitational potential (same equation).

Worked examples (≥6): E inside and outside a uniformly charged solid sphere via Gauss, then V by integrating E inward from infinity, with the plots described; the infinite line and the log potential's reference-point problem; the dipole potential and field to leading order (derive p·r̂/4πε₀r² and the field with the (3(p·r̂)r̂ − p) form); energy of the charged sphere two ways with the 3/5 factor; the parallel-plate capacitor with numbers (area, gap, voltage → charge, field, stored energy, force between plates); a conducting sphere in which a point charge sits in an off-center cavity (induced charge distribution reasoning; the external field is that of a centered charge — explain why).

Project: numerical field of N point charges on a grid; streamplot of field lines and contour plot of V for a dipole and a quadrupole; Gauss's-law checker: numerical flux through spheres and cubes around a charge distribution vs Q_enc/ε₀ (assert); solve Poisson's equation on a 2D grid by finite differences (Jacobi/Gauss–Seidel from LA-13, or scipy.sparse direct) for a parallel-plate capacitor with edge effects, compare the center field with σ/ε₀ and show the fringing; compute capacitance numerically from stored energy (ε₀/2)∫E² and compare to the ideal formula as the plate separation shrinks; energy of a charged sphere by numerical integration of E² (assert 3Q²/20πε₀R); the conductor as an equipotential: relaxation solution for V around a grounded conducting disk in a uniform field, show field lines meet it perpendicularly; the surface-charge density from the normal derivative. "Your turn": the field on the axis of a ring and a disk (closed forms) compared with the numerical superposition; a cylindrical capacitor's C vs the formula; show numerically that the field inside a uniformly charged spherical shell vanishes.

§8 honest: Gauss's law is always true and only sometimes useful — students try it on a finite rod; the ½ in the energy formula and the double-counting argument; the self-energy infinity is real and unresolved classically, don't hide it; V for infinite distributions needs a finite reference point; boundary-condition signs; the "where is the energy" question has two correct bookkeepings and students think one is wrong; finite-difference Poisson solvers converge slowly (LA-13 explains why).
