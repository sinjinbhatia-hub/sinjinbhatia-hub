# Brief: EM-7 — Electromagnetic waves: wave equation, plane waves, polarization, reflection and refraction, waveguides, dispersion
Folder: curriculum/02-physics/b-electromagnetism/07-electromagnetic-waves/ · ~12 hours · prereqs EM-6, F3 (Fourier transform) · unlocks EM-8, QM-1 (photons, blackbody), QM-2 (wave packets)
Pair with: Griffiths ch. 9; Hecht *Optics* ch. 2–4, 8 for the optics side; Feynman Lectures I ch. 28–33; MIT 8.07 notes lectures 20–24; MIT 8.03 (Vibrations and Waves) for the wave fundamentals.
Conventions: SI; complex notation Ẽ = Ẽ₀e^{i(k·r − ωt)} with physical fields the real parts (physics sign convention e^{−iωt}; note the engineering e^{jωt} and F1's warning); wave number k, angular frequency ω, c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀), index n = √(ε_rμ_r), impedance; intensity I = ⟨S⟩; polarization vector n̂ ⟂ k.

§1: Maxwell's prediction (EM-6) that c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) and Hertz's confirmation (1887); Fresnel (1821) had already derived the reflection and transmission coefficients from an elastic-ether model and gotten them right for the wrong reasons; the problem the module solves: given Maxwell's equations, what do the solutions look like, how do they interact with matter, and why does glass have a refractive index that depends on color. §2: a wave is a shape that moves without changing — f(z − vt); the student knows the 1D wave equation from PDEs and the string from F2/CM-6; a plane EM wave is E and B perpendicular to each other and to k, in phase, with E/B = c; reflection at a boundary is what the boundary conditions force; dispersion is the medium's electrons being driven oscillators (CM-1) whose response depends on frequency.

Topics in order: the 1D wave equation recap and d'Alembert's solution f(z − vt) + g(z + vt) (derive); sinusoidal waves, complex notation, the superposition principle and why complex notation is legitimate for linear equations; boundary conditions on a string (two strings joined: derive the reflection and transmission amplitudes — the template for everything that follows); polarization of transverse waves; EM WAVES IN VACUUM: from EM-6's wave equation to monochromatic plane waves; derive that E ⟂ k, B ⟂ k (from the divergence equations), B = (1/c)k̂ × E (from Faraday) — so E ⟂ B, in phase, E/B = c; the general plane wave; ENERGY AND MOMENTUM: u, S, I = ½cε₀E₀² (derive the time averages), radiation pressure P = I/c for absorption and 2I/c for reflection (derive from g = S/c²); the solar constant and the pressure of sunlight (numbers); polarization: linear, circular, elliptical; the Jones vector formalism in one page (LA: a 2-component complex vector; polarizers and wave plates as 2×2 matrices; this is a qubit's state space in disguise — QC-1 link, say so explicitly: the Poincaré sphere is the Bloch sphere); WAVES IN LINEAR MEDIA: v = c/n, n = √(ε_rμ_r), the intrinsic impedance, energy and intensity in a medium; REFLECTION AND TRANSMISSION AT NORMAL INCIDENCE: apply the boundary conditions (EM-6's four) to get r = (n₁ − n₂)/(n₁ + n₂), t = 2n₁/(n₁ + n₂) (derive), the phase flip, reflectance and transmittance R + T = 1 (derive); OBLIQUE INCIDENCE: the laws of reflection and refraction (Snell) from the requirement that the boundary conditions hold at all points and times — the k-vector matching argument (derive; compare with F6's Fermat derivation); the FRESNEL EQUATIONS for p (TM) polarization derived completely from the boundary conditions, s (TE) stated with the derivation outlined; Brewster's angle (derive tan θ_B = n₂/n₁ and why reflected light is polarized — polarized sunglasses); total internal reflection and the evanescent wave (derive the decay length; fiber optics and frustrated total internal reflection in one paragraph); ABSORPTION AND DISPERSION: Maxwell's equations in a conductor, the complex wave number, skin depth (derive δ = √(2/μσω) for a good conductor and compute it for copper at 60 Hz and 1 GHz), the phase lag between E and B in a conductor; reflection at a conducting surface (why metals are shiny); THE LORENTZ OSCILLATOR MODEL of dispersion: electrons as damped driven oscillators (CM-1's equation with the F3 transfer function), derive the complex permittivity ε(ω) = ε₀(1 + Nq²/(mε₀) Σ f_j/(ω_j² − ω² − iγ_jω)), hence n(ω) and the absorption coefficient; normal and anomalous dispersion; the Cauchy formula; why the sky is blue and sunsets red (Rayleigh scattering ∝ ω⁴ from the driven-dipole picture, one paragraph with the key derivation step); the plasma frequency and why the ionosphere reflects radio (derive n² = 1 − ω_p²/ω²); GROUP VS PHASE VELOCITY: wave packets as superpositions (F3), v_g = dω/dk derived via the stationary-phase/two-wave argument, v_g vs v_p in a dispersive medium, the spreading of a Gaussian packet (QM-2 preview: this computation IS the free-particle wave packet); GUIDED WAVES: waves between parallel conducting plates and in a rectangular waveguide — the TE_mn modes derived (separation of variables, the boundary condition E_∥ = 0), the cutoff frequency, the dispersion relation ω² = c²k² + ω_mn² and why the phase velocity exceeds c while the group velocity doesn't (compute), TEM modes impossible in a hollow guide (prove) but possible in a coax (derive); the optical fiber as a dielectric waveguide in one paragraph; a short section on antennas/radiation as the teaser for EM-8.

Worked examples (≥6): a 1 kW/m² plane wave: E₀, B₀, radiation pressure on a mirror; the Jones matrix of a quarter-wave plate turning linear into circular polarization; normal-incidence reflection from glass (n = 1.5): R = 4%; Fresnel coefficients at 45° for air→glass both polarizations with numbers; the skin depth of copper at 60 Hz and 1 GHz; the TE₁₀ cutoff and the phase/group velocities of a 2.3 × 1.0 cm X-band waveguide at 10 GHz.

"Your turn": anti-reflection coating thickness and index for a quarter-wave layer (derive, then verify with FDTD); the evanescent-wave decay length vs angle beyond the critical angle; a plasma's reflection of a pulse below the plasma frequency in FDTD.

§8 honest: complex notation hides a sign convention and the student will mix e^{−iωt} and e^{jωt} within a page; the Fresnel derivation has four boundary conditions and the trick is knowing which two are independent; "why is the sky blue" has a wrong popular answer (it's not because blue is scattered "more" in some vague sense — it's the ω⁴ law from the dipole radiation of driven electrons); group velocity can exceed c in anomalous dispersion and that does not violate relativity — the module explains why; waveguide mode indexing conventions differ; FDTD dispersion-model stability.
