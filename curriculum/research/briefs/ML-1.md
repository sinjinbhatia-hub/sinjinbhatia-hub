# Brief: ML-1 — The learning problem: ERM, loss functions, generalization, bias–variance, validation, k-NN, the curse of dimensionality
Folder: curriculum/04-machine-learning/01-the-learning-problem/ · ~10 hours · prereqs ST-1 (estimators, bias/variance/MSE), F5 · unlocks ML-2 through ML-10
Pair with: Stanford CS229 notes §1 and the "advice for applying ML" notes; Bishop & Bishop *Deep Learning: Foundations and Concepts* (free online) ch. 1; Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman ESL (free) ch. 2 and 7; Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David *Understanding Machine Learning* (free) ch. 2–4 for the formal view; Abu-Mostafa *Learning from Data* ch. 1–2.
Conventions: data (x_i, y_i) iid from an unknown P; hypothesis class H; loss ℓ(ŷ, y); empirical risk R̂(h) = (1/n)Σℓ(h(x_i), y_i); true risk R(h) = E[ℓ(h(X), Y)]; ERM ĥ = argmin_H R̂; the Bayes-optimal predictor h*; training/validation/test splits; k-fold CV; the bias–variance–noise decomposition of expected squared error.

§1: Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958), the first learning machine; Vapnik and Chervonenkis (1971) asked when minimizing training error guarantees low test error and invented statistical learning theory; Cover and Hart (1967) proved nearest-neighbor classification is at most twice the Bayes error; Bellman (1957) coined "the curse of dimensionality"; the modern practice (train/validation/test, cross-validation — Stone 1974) is the discipline that keeps learning honest. The forcing problem: statistics (ST-1) estimates parameters of a model you believe; machine learning estimates a function to predict new cases, often without believing the model, and the gap between fitting the data you have and predicting the data you don't is the entire subject. §2: a learning algorithm is a recipe that turns a dataset into a predictor; its quality is measured on data it hasn't seen; a too-flexible recipe memorizes noise (high variance), a too-rigid one can't represent the truth (high bias); the sweet spot moves with the amount of data; k-NN is the simplest recipe (average your neighbors) and the first victim of high dimensions, because in high dimensions nothing is near anything.

Topics in order: the supervised learning setup (regression, classification; features, labels, the data distribution; what "iid" buys and when it fails — ST-9's shifted-distribution warning stated); loss functions and what each optimizes for (squared loss → conditional mean, absolute → conditional median, 0–1 → the Bayes classifier, log-loss → calibrated probabilities — derive each via F5's conditional expectation/decision theory); THE BAYES-OPTIMAL PREDICTOR for regression (E[Y|X]) and classification (argmax P(Y=k|X)) with the irreducible Bayes error (derive); EMPIRICAL RISK MINIMIZATION: definition; the two questions (does R̂ approximate R? does the minimizer of one approximate the minimizer of the other?); overfitting illustrated with polynomial regression (ST-3) — training error falls monotonically with degree, test error does not; THE BIAS–VARIANCE DECOMPOSITION: E[(Y − ĥ(x))²] = noise + bias² + variance derived completely for squared loss (the ST-1 decomposition for a function at each x), what each term depends on (model class, algorithm, n), the U-shaped test curve and the classic trade-off, the modern caveat (double descent in overparameterized models — one honest paragraph with a pointer to ML-7/ML-9 where it reappears); DECOMPOSING ERROR IN PRACTICE: training error vs validation error vs test error, the generalization gap, learning curves (error vs n: high-bias and high-variance signatures — derive what each looks like and what to do about it; CS229's "advice" distilled), the data-split protocol and why the test set is touched once; VALIDATION AND CROSS-VALIDATION: hold-out, k-fold, leave-one-out (ST-3's LOO shortcut recalled), CV for model selection and the optimism of the selected model's CV score (the need for nested CV — derive the bias in a simple two-model case), the standard error of a CV estimate and the "one-standard-error rule", stratified and grouped CV, time-series CV (rolling origin; ST-7's dependence); the bootstrap's role (.632 estimator named); NEAREST NEIGHBORS: the algorithm, the distance metric (LA-1's norms; standardization matters), k as a complexity knob (k = 1 memorizes, k = n predicts the mean), the bias–variance picture for k-NN (derive the variance of a k-NN regression estimate as σ²/k and the bias growing with k), the Cover–Hart theorem stated (1-NN error ≤ 2 × Bayes error asymptotically) with the proof sketch, k-NN as a kernel smoother (the Nadaraya–Watson estimator; ML-4 preview), efficient search in one paragraph (k-d trees, approximate NN); THE CURSE OF DIMENSIONALITY, quantified: (i) the volume of a sphere vs its bounding cube goes to zero (SM-3's hypersphere formula — derive the ratio), (ii) in d dimensions, to capture a fraction r of uniform data in a cube you need an edge length r^{1/d} — compute for d = 10, 100 (nearly the whole range), (iii) the median distance to the nearest of n points in the unit ball (ESL's formula; derive) — nearest neighbors aren't near, (iv) random points are nearly equidistant (derive the concentration of pairwise distances via the CLT), (v) the number of samples to maintain density grows exponentially; what rescues learning in practice: low intrinsic dimension (manifolds; ML-6), smoothness, and parametric structure — one page that sets up the rest of the course; THE NO-FREE-LUNCH THEOREM stated and interpreted (every algorithm needs inductive bias; the bias is the prior — ST-5 link); a brief look at learning theory (ML-10 does more): finite hypothesis classes and the union bound giving the generalization bound R(ĥ) ≤ R̂(ĥ) + √(log(|H|/δ)/2n) — derive via Hoeffding's inequality (state Hoeffding; F5's concentration inequalities), the message that complexity must be paid for with data, VC dimension named; REGULARIZATION as the general trade-off knob (ST-3's ridge/lasso recalled; early stopping named — ML-7); a boxed "practical workflow" (split → baseline → learning curves → diagnose → iterate → test once) and a page on evaluation metrics for classification (accuracy's failure under class imbalance, precision/recall/F1, ROC/AUC from ST-4, calibration, confusion matrices) and regression (MSE, MAE, R² and its misuse); data leakage (the most common way to fool yourself — three examples); a closing note on what's not covered (unsupervised evaluation — ML-6; fairness and distribution shift — pointers).

Worked examples (≥7): the Bayes classifier and Bayes error for two overlapping 1D Gaussians (compute the threshold and the error); the bias–variance decomposition evaluated analytically for k-NN regression at a point with a given smooth truth; polynomial regression degree selection with training/validation curves on a given 10-point dataset (numbers); the CV standard error and the one-SE rule applied to a table of CV scores; the curse-of-dimensionality numbers (sphere/cube ratio at d = 2, 5, 10, 20; edge length for 10% of data in d = 10; median NN distance for n = 500, d = 10); the Hoeffding/union-bound generalization bound for |H| = 10⁶, n = 10⁴, δ = 0.05; the precision/recall/F1 of a classifier from a given confusion matrix and why accuracy misleads at 1% prevalence.

Project (numpy/scipy; scikit-learn allowed ONLY as a reference comparison, every algorithm implemented by hand): a synthetic-data generator with controllable noise and intrinsic dimension; k-NN regression and classification implemented (brute force), with the bias–variance decomposition estimated empirically by resampling (many training sets, same test point) across k — the U-curve with the three components plotted (assert the decomposition sums to the total MSE within Monte Carlo error); polynomial regression (ST-3's code) with train/validation/test curves vs degree and learning curves vs n for an under- and an over-parameterized model; k-fold CV implemented, nested CV vs plain CV optimism demonstrated (the selected model's plain-CV score is biased — assert the gap); the curse of dimensionality: median nearest-neighbor distance vs d (vs the formula), pairwise-distance concentration histograms at d = 2, 10, 100, k-NN accuracy collapsing with added noise dimensions on a fixed-signal problem, and recovery when the data lie on a low-dimensional manifold embedded in high d; the Hoeffding bound vs the empirical generalization gap for a finite class of threshold classifiers; the Cover–Hart bound checked on the Gaussian-mixture problem (1-NN error vs 2× Bayes error); classification metrics implemented (confusion matrix, precision, recall, F1, ROC/AUC, calibration curve) and the accuracy paradox demonstrated at 1% prevalence; a data-leakage demo (standardizing before splitting; a feature that encodes the label) showing inflated validation scores. "Your turn": the .632 bootstrap estimate of test error; a Nadaraya–Watson kernel smoother with bandwidth chosen by CV; time-series CV on an AR(1) series (ST-7) vs naive shuffled CV.

§8 honest: the bias–variance decomposition is about squared loss and the student will misapply it to classification; "overfitting" is a property of the algorithm–data pair, not of a model; the test set is a one-shot resource and students burn it by looking repeatedly; CV scores of the selected model are optimistic and almost nobody corrects for it; the curse of dimensionality is why "just add features" fails, and why ML-6's dimensionality reduction exists; the Hoeffding/union-bound argument is the only honest piece of theory at this level and it's loose by orders of magnitude — ML-10 says why; metrics without base rates are meaningless.
