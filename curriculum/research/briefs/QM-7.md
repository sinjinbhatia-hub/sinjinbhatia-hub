§1: Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen (1935) argued quantum mechanics must be incomplete because entangled particles seem to let you know "too much"; Schrödinger (1935) coined "entanglement" and called it the characteristic trait; Bohr replied unconvincingly; Bell (1964) turned the philosophy into an inequality any local hidden-variable theory must satisfy and quantum mechanics violates; Aspect (1982) and the loophole-free experiments (2015) settled it against local realism; the 2022 Nobel Prize; meanwhile Pauli's exclusion principle (1925) and the spin-statistics connection explain why matter takes up space; and Landau and von Neumann's density matrix (1927) is how you describe a system when you don't know its state or when it's part of something bigger — which is every real system. The forcing problem: one particle's state space is a Hilbert space; two particles' is the tensor product (LA-12), and most of its vectors are not products — the correlations in those vectors have no classical description, and we need both the mathematics (Schmidt decomposition, partial trace) and the physics (Bell) to handle them. §2: a two-particle state is a matrix of amplitudes (LA-12); product states are rank-1 matrices; everything else is entangled; measuring one particle "collapses" the other's description, but only the person who knows the result can use it — no faster-than-light signaling (prove it in §3); a density matrix is a probability distribution over states, or equivalently what you get when you ignore the rest of the universe; Bell's inequality is a counting argument about three coins, and quantum mechanics breaks it.
