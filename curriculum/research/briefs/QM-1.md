# Brief: QM-1 — Why quantum: the experiments that broke classical physics; the postulates previewed as a vector-space theory
Folder: curriculum/02-physics/d-quantum-mechanics/01-why-quantum/ · ~6 hours · prereqs LA-8 (spectral theorem, Hermitian operators, Dirac-notation preview), EM-8 helpful, SM-5 helpful · unlocks QM-2
Pair with: Griffiths & Schroeter *Introduction to Quantum Mechanics* 3rd ed. preface and ch. 1 opening; Feynman Lectures III ch. 1–3 (the two-slit chapters; required reading); Townsend *A Modern Approach to Quantum Mechanics* ch. 1 (Stern–Gerlach first); Eisberg & Resnick ch. 1–4 for the experiments; Zwiebach *Mastering Quantum Mechanics* ch. 1–2.
Conventions: ℏ explicit; ket |ψ⟩, bra ⟨φ|, inner product ⟨φ|ψ⟩ conjugate-linear in the first slot (LA-8's physics convention); Hermitian operators for observables; SI units with eV as the practical energy unit.

§1 IS the module: the history as a sequence of forced moves. Blackbody radiation and the ultraviolet catastrophe (SM-5; Planck 1900: energy in quanta hν); the photoelectric effect (Einstein 1905: light is particles with E = hν; the stopping-potential data); the stability of atoms (EM-8: Larmor says Rutherford's atom collapses in 10⁻¹¹ s) and the discrete spectral lines (Balmer 1885, Rydberg) with Bohr's 1913 model (quantized angular momentum L = nℏ gives the right spectrum for the wrong reasons — derive the Bohr radius and energy levels; show the Bohr–Sommerfeld rule ∮p dq = nh from CM-8 and why it couldn't be the whole story); the Compton effect (1923: photons carry momentum p = h/λ; derive the Compton shift); de Broglie (1924: if light waves are particles, particles are waves, λ = h/p; derive the Bohr condition as standing waves); Davisson–Germer and electron diffraction (1927); the Stern–Gerlach experiment (1922) and the discovery that angular momentum is quantized in a way with no classical analogue — spin — and that successive measurements along different axes destroy prior results (Townsend's opening; present the data pattern carefully: this is a qubit, QC-1); the double-slit experiment with electrons one at a time (Feynman's version; the three rules: amplitudes, not probabilities, add; which-path information kills interference; this is the "only mystery"). §2: the pattern in all of these: physical states are vectors (amplitudes add), measurement outcomes are discrete (eigenvalues), probabilities are squared amplitudes (Born), and measuring changes the state (projection). The reader who has done LA-8 should recognize the spectral theorem being described by experiments.

Topics in order (the formal content is short on purpose): the six experiments above, each with the classical prediction, the observation, and the quantum reinterpretation, with the key derivations (Bohr levels, Compton shift, de Broglie standing waves, the Stern–Gerlach deflection force F = ∇(μ·B)); the Heisenberg (1925) and Schrödinger (1926) formulations named and the fact that they were shown equivalent; THE POSTULATES PREVIEWED in vector-space language with LA-8 citations: (1) states are unit vectors |ψ⟩ in a complex inner product space (LA-8), up to a global phase; Worked examples (≥6): the Rayleigh–Jeans catastrophe vs Planck at 500 nm and 5000 K (SM-5 numbers); the photoelectric stopping potential for sodium (work function 2.3 eV) under 400 nm light; Bohr levels and the Balmer series wavelengths (compute Hα = 656 nm); the Compton shift at 90° for X-rays; the de Broglie wavelength of an electron at 100 eV vs a baseball; the full Stern–Gerlach sequence S_z → S_x → S_z with all probabilities from the Pauli-matrix eigenvectors.

Project: the Planck/Rayleigh–Jeans/Wien curves (recap, SM-5) and the photoelectric linear law from synthetic data with a fitted h; the Bohr model's spectral series plotted against the wavelength axis; a Stern–Gerlach simulator: represent the state as a 2-vector, implement measurement along an arbitrary axis n̂ as projection onto the eigenvectors of n̂·σ with Born probabilities and state collapse, run 10⁴ atoms through sequential analyzers and assert the frequencies match the predicted probabilities (including the "S_z, then S_x, then S_z gives 50/50" result); the Bloch sphere with the eigenvectors of several n̂·σ (LA-8's picture completed); a double-slit simulation: sum of two complex amplitudes vs sum of two probabilities on a detection screen, with and without "which-path" marking (modeled by orthogonal tags — a QM-7/QC preview of decoherence), showing interference appear and disappear; the uncertainty product for spin: Var(S_x)Var(S_y) vs ¼|⟨[S_x,S_y]⟩|² for random states (assert the inequality). "Your turn": the Compton shift vs angle; the Bohr model for He⁺ and the hydrogen-like scaling; a three-analyzer polarization version of Stern–Gerlach (EM-7's Jones vectors — the same mathematics).

§8 honest: the hardest part is emotional — giving up the picture of a particle having a position; the Bohr model is wrong and we teach it anyway because it got the numbers right and because the student should see exactly how it fails; "the electron is a wave" and "the electron is a particle" are both wrong statements and the module says what the right one is (the state is a vector; the measurement outcomes are what you see); the postulates are stated before the student has seen a wavefunction, which is unusual — the payoff is that QM-4 becomes a relief instead of a shock; interpretation questions will nag — park them until QM-9.
