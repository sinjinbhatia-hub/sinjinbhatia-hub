# Brief: ST-3 — Linear regression: least squares as projection, Gauss–Markov, inference, diagnostics, model selection, multicollinearity, ridge and lasso
Folder: curriculum/03-statistical-modeling/03-linear-regression/ · ~14 hours · prereqs ST-2, LA-5 (projections, QR), LA-9 (SVD, condition number) · unlocks ST-4, ST-7, ML-3
Pair with: Agresti *Foundations of Linear and Generalized Linear Models* ch. 1–3; Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman *The Elements of Statistical Learning* (free) ch. 3; Stanford STATS 305A (course notes online); Wasserman ch. 13; Gelman, Hill & Vehtari *Regression and Other Stories* ch. 6–12 for the applied judgment; Strang 18.06 lectures 15–16 for the projection picture.
Conventions: y = Xβ + ε with y ∈ R^n, X n×p (first column of ones for the intercept), ε ~ (0, σ²I); β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy; hat matrix H = X(XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ; residuals e = y − ŷ = (I − H)y; RSS, TSS, R²; σ̂² = RSS/(n − p); standardized predictors where stated; ridge penalty λ‖β‖², lasso penalty λ‖β‖₁.

§1: Legendre (1805) and Gauss (1809) invented least squares for orbits (LA-5's story), Gauss proved the Gauss–Markov theorem (1821), Galton and Pearson (1886–1900) turned it into "regression" for heredity data, Fisher gave it its inference theory (1922–25), and Hoerl & Kennard (1970) and Tibshirani (1996) added the penalties that made it work with many predictors; the forcing problem: you have a response and several predictors measured with noise, and you want to know how the response depends on them, how sure you are, and which predictors matter — and the geometry of LA-5 turns out to answer the first and the probability of ST-1/2 the rest. §2: the fitted values are the projection of y onto the column space of X (LA-5's picture, now with statistical meaning); the residual is perpendicular to every predictor; the coefficient's uncertainty depends on how far that predictor's direction is from the span of the others (multicollinearity = nearly dependent columns = LA-9's condition number); R² is the squared cosine of the angle between y and its projection; ridge shrinks coefficients along directions of small singular value (LA-9's SVD picture), lasso shrinks some to exactly zero.

Topics in order: the simple linear regression model and its least-squares solution (derive β̂₁ = Sxy/Sxx; the regression line through the means; regression to the mean — F5's conditional expectation made empirical); THE MULTIPLE REGRESSION MODEL in matrix form; LEAST SQUARES AS PROJECTION: derive the normal equations by geometry (LA-5) and by calculus (LA-14), β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy, the hat matrix and its properties (symmetric, idempotent, trace p — prove), ŷ = Hy, e = (I − H)y, e ⟂ C(X) (prove), the ANOVA decomposition TSS = ESS + RSS (prove via Pythagoras in LA-5; the geometry of R²), R² and adjusted R² (why adjusted); interpretation of coefficients (partial effects; "holding others fixed"), the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem (prove: the coefficient on x_j equals the simple regression of residualized y on residualized x_j — the cleanest explanation of what "controlling for" means); THE GAUSS–MARKOV THEOREM: under E[ε] = 0, Var(ε) = σ²I, β̂ is BLUE — full proof (any other linear unbiased estimator is β̂ plus an uncorrelated piece; Var adds); E[β̂] = β and Var(β̂) = σ²(XᵀX)⁻¹ derived; §8 honest: "controlling for" is what the FWL theorem says and nothing more — it does not make a coefficient causal; the normal equations are the wrong way to compute OLS (LA-5/13) and every student does it anyway; R² is not a measure of model quality and students worship it; the prediction interval vs the confidence interval for the mean is confused constantly; leverage is about x, outlyingness about y, influence about both; multicollinearity does not bias estimates — it inflates variance, and the SVD picture is the only one that makes that obvious; ridge and lasso need standardized predictors and students forget; post-selection inference is a swamp that this module can only point at; the lasso's exact zeros are a property of the ℓ¹ ball's corners, not magic.
