# Brief: ST-1 — Inference foundations: statistical models, estimators, bias/variance/MSE, consistency, sufficiency, MLE, method of moments, Fisher information, Cramér–Rao
Folder: curriculum/03-statistical-modeling/01-inference-foundations/ · ~12 hours · prereqs F5 (joint distributions, CLT, KL), LA-14 (gradients, Hessians) · unlocks ST-2, ML-1
Pair with: Wasserman *All of Statistics* ch. 6–7, 9; MIT 18.650 (Rigollet) ch. 1–3 (OCW); Casella & Berger ch. 6–7 (reference); Rice *Mathematical Statistics and Data Analysis* ch. 8; Efron & Hastie *Computer Age Statistical Inference* ch. 1–4 (free PDF) for the big picture.
Conventions: data X₁..X_n iid from p(x; θ), θ ∈ Θ; estimator θ̂_n = T(X₁..X_n) (a random variable); true value θ₀; log-likelihood ℓ(θ) = Σ log p(X_i; θ); score s(θ) = ∇ℓ; Fisher information I(θ) = E[s sᵀ] = −E[∇²ℓ] (per sample: I₁; for n samples: nI₁); bias, variance, MSE; convergence in probability →ᵖ, in distribution →ᵈ.

§1: Gauss (1809) and Laplace used least squares and the normal law; Fisher (1912–1925) invented maximum likelihood, sufficiency, efficiency, and information, essentially creating the subject in a decade; Neyman and Pearson, Wald, Cramér and Rao (1945) gave it its optimality theory; the forcing problem: given data from a distribution with unknown parameters, how do you estimate them, how good is your estimate, and is there a best possible estimate? Probability (F4/F5) goes from model to data; inference goes from data to model, and needs its own concepts (an estimator is a random variable; its sampling distribution is what we study). §2: an estimator is a recipe; apply it to many hypothetical datasets and it has a distribution; bias is where that distribution is centered relative to the truth, variance is how spread it is; MSE = bias² + variance (derive); the likelihood is "how surprised would this parameter be by this data", and maximizing it picks the least-surprised parameter; Fisher information is the curvature of the log-likelihood at the peak — a sharp peak means the data pin the parameter down — and no unbiased estimator can beat 1/(curvature) (Cramér–Rao).

Topics in order: statistical models (parametric, nonparametric; iid), point estimation, the sampling distribution of an estimator, BIAS/VARIANCE/MSE with the decomposition derived, consistency (θ̂_n →ᵖ θ₀; via Chebyshev when MSE → 0 — prove), the sample mean and sample variance as the running example (prove E[S²] = σ² for the n−1 version; derive the variance of the sample variance for normal data; the MLE of σ² is biased and has lower MSE — compute both, the bias–variance trade-off's first appearance); THE METHOD OF MOMENTS: match sample moments to population moments, solve (derive for Normal, Gamma, Uniform(0,θ)); consistency by the law of large numbers; MAXIMUM LIKELIHOOD: the likelihood function, the log-likelihood, the MLE as the maximizer, invariance (prove: the MLE of g(θ) is g(θ̂)), examples derived fully: Bernoulli, Poisson, Exponential, Normal (μ, σ² jointly — LA-14's gradient identities), Uniform(0, θ) (where calculus fails and the MLE is the maximum — a warning case), the multivariate Normal (LA-14's log det and inverse identities; the MLE covariance), Gamma (no closed form — numerical, the project does it); THE SCORE and its properties: E[s(θ₀)] = 0 (derive via differentiating ∫p = 1 under the integral), Var[s] = I(θ) (derive), the two forms of Fisher information and their equality (derive); FISHER INFORMATION computed for the standard families (Bernoulli: 1/(p(1−p)); Normal mean: 1/σ²; Poisson: 1/λ; Exponential; the Normal (μ, σ²) matrix); information adds over independent samples (prove); reparametrization of I (derive the Jacobian rule — LA-14); THE CRAMÉR–RAO BOUND: for an unbiased estimator, Var(θ̂) ≥ 1/(nI₁(θ)) — full proof via Cauchy–Schwarz (LA-1/LA-8: Cov(θ̂, s)² ≤ Var(θ̂)Var(s) with Cov(θ̂, s) = 1 derived by differentiating E[θ̂] = θ), the biased version stated, the multiparameter version Var(θ̂) ⪰ I⁻¹ stated (LA-8 PSD ordering); EFFICIENCY: an estimator achieving the bound; the sample mean is efficient for the Normal mean (check); the sample variance is not efficient for σ² (compute the ratio); when the bound is attainable (exponential families; derive the condition from equality in Cauchy–Schwarz: the score must be affine in the estimator); ASYMPTOTICS OF THE MLE: consistency (sketch via the law of large numbers applied to the log-likelihood and the KL divergence — F5: E[log p(X;θ)] is maximized at θ₀ because D(p_θ₀‖p_θ) ≥ 0 — derive this identification, it's the conceptual core), asymptotic normality √n(θ̂ − θ₀) →ᵈ N(0, 1/I₁(θ₀)) — derive via the Taylor expansion of the score around θ₀, the law of large numbers for the Hessian, and the CLT for the score (the standard heuristic proof; be honest about regularity conditions), so the MLE is asymptotically efficient (achieves Cramér–Rao); the delta method (derive via Taylor; apply to the MLE of a function); the Wald standard error se(θ̂) ≈ 1/√(nI₁(θ̂)) and the observed information alternative; a worked check: the Bernoulli MLE's asymptotic distribution vs the exact Binomial; SUFFICIENCY: definition, the factorization theorem (prove the discrete case), sufficient statistics for the standard families (Σx_i for Poisson/Bernoulli/Exponential; (Σx_i, Σx_i²) for Normal; max for Uniform), minimal sufficiency in one paragraph, the Rao–Blackwell theorem (prove: conditioning an estimator on a sufficient statistic never increases MSE — F5's Eve's law) with one worked improvement; EXPONENTIAL FAMILIES: the form p(x;θ) = h(x)exp(η(θ)ᵀT(x) − A(η)), the examples rewritten in this form (Bernoulli, Poisson, Normal, Gamma, Exponential), the log-partition function A and its derivatives giving the mean and variance of T (derive — SM-4's ln Z and its derivatives: the same object; say so), the MLE in an exponential family solves E_θ[T] = T̄ (derive: moment matching), concavity of the log-likelihood in η (prove via the PSD Hessian — LA-8/14), the Fisher information as ∇²A, and the maximum-entropy characterization (F5/SM-4: exponential families are the max-ent distributions with constraints on E[T] — state with the derivation outline) — this section is the unifying device for ST-4 (GLMs) and ST-5 (conjugate priors); BAYESIAN ESTIMATION previewed in one page: the posterior ∝ likelihood × prior, the posterior mean as an estimator, its bias and shrinkage, and the statement that it's the subject of ST-5; a page on what can go wrong: non-identifiability, the MLE not existing or being on the boundary, multiple maxima, the Neyman–Scott problem (MLE inconsistent with many nuisance parameters — state), and model misspecification (the MLE converges to the KL-closest model — derive from the KL identification above).

Worked examples (≥7): MSE of the two variance estimators for Normal data with n = 10; MoM and MLE for Gamma(α, β) (MoM in closed form; MLE requires the digamma equation — set it up); the MLE and Fisher information for Bernoulli with the Cramér–Rao bound attained; the Normal (μ, σ²) joint MLE via LA-14 with the Fisher information matrix and its inverse; the Uniform(0, θ) MLE θ̂ = max, its bias (nθ/(n+1)), the unbiased correction, and why Cramér–Rao doesn't apply (regularity fails); the asymptotic normality of the Poisson MLE and a 95% Wald interval for a given sample; Rao–Blackwellizing the estimator "X₁" for the Poisson mean (recovers the sample mean).

§8 honest: the single hardest conceptual step is that an estimator is a random variable with a distribution, and "the" standard error is the standard deviation of that distribution — students compute standard errors without knowing what's random; MLE is not always unbiased, not always consistent, and not always computable, and the Uniform example exists to show the regularity conditions matter; the Cramér–Rao proof is Cauchy–Schwarz and everyone forgets which covariance equals 1; the asymptotic-normality derivation hides a regularity assumption at every step; sufficiency feels like a formality until Rao–Blackwell and the exponential-family section use it; the exponential-family section is dense and pays off three modules later — read it twice.
