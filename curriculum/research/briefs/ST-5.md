# Brief: ST-5 — Bayesian inference I: priors, posteriors, conjugacy, credible intervals, predictive distributions, Bayesian regression, model comparison
Folder: curriculum/03-statistical-modeling/05-bayesian-inference-1/ · ~12 hours · prereq ST-4 (exponential families), F5 (Beta–Binomial, MVN conditionals) · unlocks ST-6, ML-4 (Gaussian processes), ML-10
Pair with: Gelman, Carlin, Stern, Dunson, Vehtari & Rubin *Bayesian Data Analysis* 3rd ed. (free PDF) ch. 1–5, 14; McElreath *Statistical Rethinking* 2nd ed. ch. 1–4, 7; Murphy *Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction* ch. 4; Jaynes *Probability Theory: The Logic of Science* ch. 1–4 for the philosophy; Hoff *A First Course in Bayesian Statistical Methods* for the clearest conjugate derivations.
Conventions: prior p(θ), likelihood p(y|θ), posterior p(θ|y) ∝ p(y|θ)p(θ), marginal likelihood/evidence p(y) = ∫p(y|θ)p(θ)dθ, posterior predictive p(ỹ|y) = ∫p(ỹ|θ)p(θ|y)dθ; conjugate families; hyperparameters in Greek with subscript 0 (α₀, β₀, μ₀, κ₀); credible interval vs ST-2's confidence interval; Bayes factor BF₁₀ = p(y|M₁)/p(y|M₀).

§1: Bayes (1763, published posthumously) and Laplace (1774, independently and more generally) inverted probability to reason from effects to causes; Laplace used it for everything from Saturn's mass to the proportion of male births; the frequentist revolt (Fisher, Neyman) made "inverse probability" disreputable for most of the 20th century because priors seemed subjective; Jeffreys (1939), Savage, de Finetti, Lindley, and Jaynes kept it alive; computation (MCMC, 1990s; ST-6) made it practical; today it is the working method in astrophysics, genomics, forecasting, and machine learning. The forcing problem: frequentist inference answers "how would this procedure behave over repetitions" but not "what should I believe about θ given this data"; many real decisions need the second question answered, with prior knowledge incorporated and uncertainty propagated through to predictions — and the only coherent way to do that is probability over parameters. §2: the posterior is the prior reweighted by the likelihood — the picture of two curves multiplying; data sharpens belief; a conjugate prior is one whose shape the likelihood preserves, so updating is just updating hyperparameters (counts added to pseudo-counts); the posterior predictive is "average the predictions over what you still don't know about θ"; model comparison asks which model predicted the data better before seeing it, which automatically penalizes complexity.

Topics in order: Bayes' theorem for parameters (F4's theorem with densities), the three distributions (prior, likelihood, posterior) and the two integrals (evidence, predictive); THE BETA–BINOMIAL MODEL in full: Beta(α₀, β₀) prior × Binomial likelihood → Beta(α₀ + y, β₀ + n − y) (derive; F5's conjugacy), the posterior mean as a weighted average of the prior mean and the MLE (derive the weights — shrinkage), the posterior variance shrinking as 1/n, the effect of prior strength (α₀ + β₀ as a pseudo-sample-size), the predictive distribution (Beta-Binomial; derive), Laplace's rule of succession as the uniform-prior case (derive (y+1)/(n+2)); THE NORMAL MODEL: known variance, Normal prior on μ → Normal posterior with precision-weighted mean (derive; precisions add — the F5 MVN conditional in disguise), the posterior for σ² with μ known (Inverse-Gamma/scaled-inverse-χ²; derive), the joint Normal–Inverse-Gamma conjugate prior for (μ, σ²) (derive the posterior updates; the marginal posterior of μ is a t — derive, and compare with ST-2's t interval: the uninformative-prior Bayesian interval coincides numerically with the frequentist one, with a different interpretation); THE POISSON–GAMMA model (derive; the predictive is Negative Binomial — ST-4's overdispersion from a Bayesian angle); the general EXPONENTIAL FAMILY conjugate prior (ST-1/ST-4): for likelihood exp(ηᵀT(y) − A(η)), the conjugate prior is exp(ηᵀχ − νA(η)) and the update is χ → χ + ΣT(y_i), ν → ν + n (derive; show it reproduces the three cases above; the hyperparameters as prior sufficient statistics and prior sample size — the unifying insight of the module); PRIORS: informative, weakly informative, "noninformative" (flat priors are not invariant under reparametrization — derive the Jacobian problem with the Bernoulli p vs logit example), Jeffreys' prior ∝ √I(θ) (ST-1's Fisher information; derive its invariance; compute it for Bernoulli (Beta(½,½)), Poisson, Normal mean and scale), improper priors and when they're OK, reference and maximum-entropy priors named (F5/SM-4), the modern practice of weakly informative priors (Gelman's recommendations) and prior predictive checks (simulate data from the prior and look — McElreath's method); CREDIBLE INTERVALS: equal-tailed and highest-posterior-density (define; compute for a Beta posterior), the interpretation (a probability statement about θ — the thing ST-2 said a confidence interval is NOT), the asymptotic agreement with confidence intervals (the Bernstein–von Mises theorem stated: the posterior → N(θ̂, 1/(nI)) — ST-1's asymptotic normality in Bayesian clothing), and the disagreement for small n or strong priors; POINT ESTIMATES AND DECISIONS: posterior mean (minimizes expected squared error — prove), median (absolute error), mode/MAP (0–1 loss; and MAP as penalized MLE — ST-3's ridge is MAP with a Normal prior, lasso with a Laplace prior — derive both), the decision-theoretic framing in one page (loss, risk, Bayes risk); THE POSTERIOR PREDICTIVE and its uses: prediction with full uncertainty, posterior predictive checks (simulate replicated data, compare to the observed — BDA's model checking), the Normal predictive (a t; derive); BAYESIAN LINEAR REGRESSION: the Normal prior β ~ N(μ₀, Σ₀) with known σ² → Normal posterior (derive via completing the square — F5's MVN conditional once more; the posterior mean as a ridge estimate — ST-3 — with Σ₀ = σ²/λ I), the predictive distribution for a new x (derive the mean and variance; the variance grows away from the data — the Gaussian-process picture of ML-4 previewed), the unknown-σ² case (Normal–Inverse-Gamma; the predictive is a t), the g-prior and Zellner in one paragraph, Bayesian logistic regression (no conjugacy; the Laplace approximation to the posterior derived via LA-14's Hessian at the MAP — the bridge to ST-6 and ML-4), hierarchical priors previewed (ST-6); MODEL COMPARISON: the marginal likelihood as "how well did the model predict the data before seeing it", Bayes factors, their automatic Occam penalty (derive the Occam factor for a Normal model: the evidence ≈ max likelihood × (posterior width / prior width) — MacKay's argument), the Jeffreys–Lindley paradox (a Bayes factor can favor H₀ while a p-value rejects it; derive in the Normal mean case and explain), sensitivity of Bayes factors to priors (the honest problem), BIC as the large-n approximation to −2 log evidence (derive via the Laplace approximation), information criteria for Bayesian models (WAIC and LOO named; ST-6 computes them), and the alternative of Bayesian model averaging in one paragraph; a closing page: the frequentist–Bayesian relationship as two valid answers to two different questions, where they agree (large n), where they don't (small n, strong priors, stopping rules — the likelihood principle in one paragraph), and the pragmatic position the course takes.

Worked examples (≥8): Beta–Binomial with a Beta(2,2) prior and 7 successes in 10: posterior, mean, 95% credible interval, predictive probability of success on the next trial; the Normal mean with a Normal prior: precision-weighted update with numbers; the Normal–Inverse-Gamma posterior for 10 measurements and the t marginal compared with ST-2's interval; the exponential-family conjugate update reproducing Poisson–Gamma; Jeffreys' prior for Bernoulli and Poisson derived; MAP = ridge derived with the λ ↔ prior variance correspondence; Bayesian regression on 5 data points with the predictive variance at an interpolation and an extrapolation point; the Jeffreys–Lindley paradox with n = 10⁴, x̄ = 0.02, σ = 1 (p ≈ 0.045; Bayes factor favors H₀).

§8 honest: "the prior is subjective" is both true and the wrong objection — the likelihood is a modeling choice too, and the module says what's actually at stake (small-n sensitivity, Bayes-factor fragility); credible and confidence intervals are different answers to different questions, and the student who "understood" ST-2 will have to hold both at once; completing the square for the Normal–Normal and Bayesian-regression posteriors is the same LA/F5 computation for the fourth time — if it's still painful, that's the signal to redo F5; flat priors are not noninformative; Bayes factors depend on priors in ways posteriors don't, and model comparison is the least settled part of the field; MAP estimates are not Bayesian in spirit (they ignore the posterior's shape) even though they're computed with a prior.
