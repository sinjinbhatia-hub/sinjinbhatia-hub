Conventions: layer ℓ with weights W^{(ℓ)} (out×in), biases b^{(ℓ)}, pre-activation z^{(ℓ)} = W^{(ℓ)}a^{(ℓ−1)} + b^{(ℓ)}, activation a^{(ℓ)} = φ(z^{(ℓ)}); batch as rows of X (n×d) so Z = AW^T + 1b^T (state which convention and stick to it); loss L; the backprop error δ^{(ℓ)} = ∂L/∂z^{(ℓ)}; activations ReLU, sigmoid, tanh, GELU; fan-in/fan-out; batch norm parameters γ, β; dropout rate p.

§1: McCulloch and Pitts (1943) and Rosenblatt (1958) built the neuron; Minsky and Papert (1969) killed the single layer; Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams (1986) revived multilayer networks with backpropagation (Werbos 1974, Linnainmaa 1970 had the algorithm); Cybenko (1989) and Hornik (1991) proved universal approximation; the second winter ended with Hinton's 2006 pretraining, then GPUs, ReLU (Nair & Hinton 2010; Glorot 2011), good initialization (Glorot 2010; He 2015), batch normalization (2015), Adam (2014), and AlexNet (2012) — none of which is deep theory, all of which is why it works now. The forcing problem: linear models need good features (ML-3), kernels need a good kernel (ML-4), trees need the right splits (ML-5); a neural network learns the features, the kernel, and the splits from data by composing simple nonlinear maps and training the whole thing by gradient descent — and the entire engineering of the field is about making that gradient descent work. §2: a layer is a linear map (LA-4) followed by a pointwise nonlinearity; stacking layers composes functions; the universal approximation theorem says one wide-enough hidden layer can represent any continuous function (a sum of bumps), but depth composes features hierarchically and is exponentially more efficient for some functions; backpropagation is LA-14's chain rule evaluated from the output backward, so every parameter's gradient costs about one forward pass; the practical obstacles — gradients that vanish or explode through depth — are LA-7's eigenvalue products, and initialization and normalization are the fixes.
