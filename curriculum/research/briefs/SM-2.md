# Brief: SM-2 — Thermodynamics II: entropy, the second law, engines, thermodynamic potentials, Maxwell relations, Legendre transforms
Folder: curriculum/02-physics/c-thermo-statistical-mechanics/02-thermodynamics-2/ · ~10 hours · prereq SM-1 · unlocks SM-3, CM-8 (Legendre transform parallel)
Pair with: Schroeder ch. 4–5; Callen *Thermodynamics and an Introduction to Thermostatistics* ch. 1–7 (the axiomatic version, for the structure); Fermi ch. 3–5; Feynman Lectures I ch. 44–46.
Conventions: as SM-1; entropy S with dS = đQ_rev/T; thermodynamic potentials U(S,V,N), H(S,P,N), F(T,V,N), G(T,P,N); chemical potential μ = (∂U/∂N)_{S,V}; the thermodynamic identity dU = TdS − PdV + μdN.

§1: Carnot (1824) proved no engine beats a reversible one without knowing why; Clausius (1865) found the state function that Carnot's argument implied and named it entropy; Kelvin and Clausius gave the second law its two equivalent forms; Gibbs (1873–78) built the entire structure of thermodynamic potentials and phase equilibria from them, essentially as geometry; the forcing problem: energy conservation cannot explain why heat flows hot to cold, why engines have a maximum efficiency, or why anything is irreversible — a second, independent law is needed, and its content is a new state function. §2: entropy as "the number of ways" previewed in words (SM-3 makes it precise), so heat flowing from hot to cold increases the total count; temperature as the thing that equalizes when entropy is maximized (1/T = ∂S/∂U); free energy as the energy you can actually extract when the environment holds T fixed; a Legendre transform as "stop describing the system by its entropy and start describing it by the temperature of the bath it's in" (CM-8 did the same with velocity → momentum).

Topics in order: the Carnot cycle (the four legs for an ideal gas; derive the efficiency 1 − T_c/T_h using SM-1's adiabatic and isothermal results); CARNOT'S THEOREM (no engine between two reservoirs beats a reversible one; all reversible ones have the same efficiency — prove by the composite-engine argument); the Kelvin and Clausius statements of the second law and their equivalence (prove both directions); the CLAUSIUS INEQUALITY ∮đQ/T ≤ 0 (derive from Carnot's theorem via the standard decomposition into Carnot cycles) and hence the existence of the state function S with dS = đQ_rev/T (derive), and ΔS ≥ ∫đQ/T for irreversible processes; the second law as ΔS_universe ≥ 0; ENTROPY CHANGES computed: heating at constant volume, isothermal expansion, free expansion (the same ΔS as isothermal expansion — explain why, and that S increased with no heat flow), mixing of two gases (and the Gibbs paradox stated, resolved in SM-3), heat flow between two bodies (ΔS_total > 0 — derive), melting; the THERMODYNAMIC IDENTITY dU = TdS − PdV (+ μdN) and the definitions T = (∂U/∂S)_V, P = −(∂U/∂V)_S, μ = (∂U/∂N)_{S,V}; the entropy of an ideal gas S(U, V, N) up to a constant (derive by integrating; the Sackur–Tetrode constant deferred to SM-3); the third law stated; ENGINES AND REFRIGERATORS: efficiencies and COPs, the Otto and Diesel cycle efficiencies derived (finishing SM-1's example), real-engine losses in one paragraph, heat pumps and why they beat resistive heating (numbers); THERMODYNAMIC POTENTIALS via LEGENDRE TRANSFORMS: the convexity argument and the construction (CM-8's definition restated; show S(U) concave ⇔ positive heat capacity), H = U + PV, F = U − TS, G = U − TS + PV with their natural variables and differentials (derive each dH, dF, dG); why each is useful: F is the maximum work extractable at constant T (derive W_other ≤ −ΔF), G is the quantity minimized at constant T and P (derive from the second law applied to system + reservoir — this is the argument that makes chemistry and phase transitions go), H for constant-pressure heat; the MAXWELL RELATIONS: derive all four from the symmetry of second derivatives (∂²/∂x∂y), with the mnemonic; applications: (∂S/∂V)_T = (∂P/∂T)_V used to get the entropy of a van der Waals gas; C_P − C_V in general = TVα²/κ_T (derive — the showpiece calculation using the cyclic rule and a Maxwell relation); the Joule–Thomson coefficient (derive; why gases cool on throttling below the inversion temperature; liquefaction); the chemical potential introduced carefully: μ as the energy to add a particle at fixed S and V, equivalently (∂G/∂N)_{T,P}, so G = μN for a pure substance (derive via extensivity/Euler's theorem on homogeneous functions — CM-4's tool), μ of an ideal gas μ = k_BT ln(P/P₀) + const (derive); PHASE EQUILIBRIUM: coexistence requires equal μ (derive from minimizing G), the Clausius–Clapeyron equation dP/dT = L/(TΔV) (derive), applied to water's boiling point vs altitude; the phase diagram of water in words; the van der Waals liquid–gas transition and Maxwell's equal-area construction (derive the construction from equal μ; SM-6 revisits as a mean-field theory); a closing section "what thermodynamics cannot tell you" (the values of C_V, the equation of state, the constant in S, why the second law holds) as the motivation for SM-3.

Worked examples (≥6): Carnot efficiency between 500 K and 300 K and the entropy bookkeeping for one cycle; ΔS for a free expansion of 1 mol by a factor of 2 and for the isothermal reversible version; two bricks at 400 K and 200 K brought into contact: final T and ΔS_total; the Legendre transform from U(S,V) to F(T,V) for an ideal gas explicitly (both functions written out); C_P − C_V = TVα²/κ_T evaluated for liquid water (compare with the tabulated difference); Clausius–Clapeyron for water: boiling point at 3000 m altitude.

Project: a numerical Carnot cycle for the SM-1 hard-disk gas (or an ideal gas model with explicit heat baths via velocity rescaling) with the efficiency measured vs 1 − T_c/T_h; entropy production: two subsystems exchanging energy, ΔS_total computed from S(U) for each and shown ≥ 0 for every direction of heat flow except the equilibrium one; Maxwell relations verified symbolically with sympy for the ideal gas and van der Waals gas (second derivatives commute); the Legendre transform computed numerically (max over S of TS − U(S)) for a tabulated U(S) and compared with the closed form F(T); Maxwell's equal-area construction for van der Waals CO₂ at several temperatures below T_c, with the coexistence pressures found by root-finding and the resulting coexistence curve plotted; Clausius–Clapeyron integrated numerically for water with a temperature-dependent L vs the simple exponential approximation; the Joule–Thomson inversion curve for a van der Waals gas. "Your turn": the efficiency of a Stirling cycle; the entropy of mixing for two gases vs identical gases (the Gibbs paradox numerically); a heat pump's COP vs outdoor temperature.

§8 honest: the second law has a dozen equivalent statements and students treat them as different laws; "entropy is disorder" is a slogan that will mislead you — SM-3 gives the real definition; the free-expansion entropy increase without heat flow is the test of whether you understand that S is a state function; the Legendre-transform direction (which variable is traded for which) is the same confusion as in CM-8; Maxwell relations are trivial to derive and students memorize them instead; chemical potential is introduced here and will confuse you until SM-5 — that's normal and the module says so; Clausius–Clapeyron sign conventions.
