# Brief: ST-9 — Causal inference and experimental design: randomization, confounding, DAGs, adjustment, instruments, the limits of regression
Folder: curriculum/03-statistical-modeling/09-causal-inference/ · ~8 hours · prereqs ST-4, ST-5 helpful, F4 (conditional probability, Simpson's paradox) · unlocks the honest use of every other module in this course
Pair with: McElreath *Statistical Rethinking* ch. 5–6 (the clearest DAG introduction); Hernán & Robins *Causal Inference: What If* (free PDF) ch. 1–7; Pearl, Glymour & Jewell *Causal Inference in Statistics: A Primer* ch. 1–3; Angrist & Pischke *Mostly Harmless Econometrics* ch. 2–4 for the potential-outcomes/econometrics view; Cunningham *Causal Inference: The Mixtape* (free online) for examples; Fisher *The Design of Experiments* ch. 2 for the original randomization argument.
Conventions: treatment A (binary unless stated), outcome Y, covariates L; potential outcomes Y^{a=1}, Y^{a=0}; individual causal effect Y^{a=1} − Y^{a=0}; average treatment effect ATE = E[Y^{a=1}] − E[Y^{a=0}]; association E[Y|A=1] − E[Y|A=0]; the do-operator P(Y|do(A=a)); DAG conventions (nodes, arrows, confounder/fork, mediator/chain, collider); exchangeability Y^a ⊥ A (| L); the backdoor criterion; instrument Z.

§1: Fisher (1925) introduced randomization as the thing that makes causal conclusions valid; Neyman (1923) wrote down potential outcomes for agricultural experiments, and Rubin (1974) revived them for observational data; Wright (1921) drew path diagrams for guinea-pig genetics, and Pearl (1988–2000) turned them into the DAG calculus; Hill (1965) laid out the criteria for inferring that smoking causes cancer without a randomized trial; the forcing problem: every module so far estimates associations, and the questions people actually ask — does this drug work, does this policy help, does this design change improve the product — are causal; the gap between association and causation is not a slogan but a precise, formal difference, and closing it requires either randomization or untestable assumptions stated out loud. §2: a causal effect is a comparison of two worlds for the same unit (the one where it was treated and the one where it wasn't), only one of which happens — the fundamental problem; randomization makes the treated and untreated groups exchangeable so the average over groups equals the average over worlds; a confounder is a common cause that makes groups non-exchangeable; a DAG is a drawing of which variables cause which, and from it you can read off which variables to adjust for (and which ones adjusting for makes things WORSE — colliders); regression "controls for" a variable in the FWL sense (ST-3), which is the right thing only if the DAG says so.

Topics in order: association vs causation made precise: Simpson's paradox (F4) as a causal problem — the same data supports opposite conclusions depending on the causal structure (do the kidney-stone or the UC Berkeley admissions example with the numbers and both DAGs); POTENTIAL OUTCOMES: definitions, the fundamental problem of causal inference (only one potential outcome is observed), consistency (Y = Y^A), the individual vs average effect, why E[Y|A=1] − E[Y|A=0] ≠ ATE in general — derive the decomposition into ATE + selection bias (the observed difference = E[Y^1 − Y^0|A=1] + (E[Y^0|A=1] − E[Y^0|A=0]) — the selection-bias term is the whole problem); RANDOMIZED EXPERIMENTS: randomization ⇒ exchangeability Y^a ⊥ A ⇒ the association is the ATE (prove), Fisher's exact randomization test (the sharp null, re-randomize to get the null distribution — ST-2's permutation test is this), Neyman's variance for the difference in means (derive; the conservative bound), blocking and stratification (why they help: variance reduction, derived for a simple case), factorial designs in one page (main effects and interactions; the 2^k design; why it beats one-factor-at-a-time — ME relevance: this is DOE), the ME/engineering connection to design of experiments (Taguchi named; response surfaces named), A/B testing as the modern RCT (sample size via ST-2's power formula; the peeking problem — ST-2's optional stopping), noncompliance and intention-to-treat; OBSERVATIONAL STUDIES and CONDITIONAL EXCHANGEABILITY: the assumption Y^a ⊥ A | L (no unmeasured confounding), STANDARDIZATION (the g-formula: E[Y^a] = Σ_l E[Y|A=a, L=l]P(L=l) — derive from conditional exchangeability and consistency) and INVERSE PROBABILITY WEIGHTING (E[Y^a] = E[I(A=a)Y/P(A=a|L)] — derive; propensity scores; the equivalence of the two in the saturated case — prove), positivity, the propensity score theorem (conditioning on P(A=1|L) suffices — state with the proof sketch), matching in one paragraph, doubly robust estimators named; DAGs: nodes, edges, paths, the three structures — fork (confounder: A ← L → Y), chain (mediator: A → M → Y), collider (A → C ← Y) — and what conditioning does to each (derive the d-separation rules; the collider case with a simulation: conditioning on a collider CREATES association — Berkson's paradox; the "beauty and talent" or hospital example), d-separation defined, THE BACKDOOR CRITERION (a set L blocks every backdoor path from A to Y and contains no descendant of A ⇒ adjusting for L identifies the effect — state, prove in the simplest case, apply to five DAGs including ones where adjusting for an available variable is wrong), the front-door criterion stated, M-bias and the "adjust for everything" fallacy, mediation (total vs direct effects; the mediator fallacy of conditioning on a post-treatment variable — derive the bias), selection bias as collider bias (the survivorship/case-selection examples), measurement error as a confounding-like problem in one paragraph; REGRESSION AND CAUSATION: when a regression coefficient is a causal effect (linear model + backdoor set in the regressors + no post-treatment variables + correct functional form — list and justify each), the omitted-variable bias formula (ST-3) as the confounding formula, "controlling for" revisited via FWL, "bad controls" (colliders and mediators) with the derivation of the sign of the bias in a linear DAG (Wright's path rules: the covariance between two variables is the sum over open paths of the products of path coefficients — derive for a 3-node fork and a 3-node collider and use it to compute the bias of the wrong regression exactly), the table-2 fallacy; INSTRUMENTAL VARIABLES: the setup (Z affects A, affects Y only through A, shares no cause with Y — the DAG), the Wald estimator for a binary instrument (derive: the ratio of reduced-form to first-stage effects), two-stage least squares (derive as the IV estimator and as a projection — LA-5: project A onto Z, then regress Y on the projection; the standard-error correction), the LATE interpretation under monotonicity (state; the compliers), weak instruments (the bias toward OLS and the F > 10 rule of thumb), the classic examples (draft lottery, distance to college, rainfall) with their assumptions critiqued; other designs in one paragraph each: regression discontinuity (local randomization at a threshold; the bandwidth trade-off), difference-in-differences (parallel trends; the 2×2 table and the regression form; event-study plots), synthetic control named; SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS: how strong would an unmeasured confounder have to be to explain the effect away (Rosenbaum bounds / the E-value idea; derive the simple linear-bias version); causal discovery from data in one paragraph (what's identifiable from observational data: the Markov equivalence class; why you can't learn arrow directions from correlations alone except at colliders); CAUSAL QUESTIONS IN THE OTHER MODULES: ST-3's regression (associations unless the DAG justifies more), ST-7's Granger causality (predictive, not causal — explain), ML's predictive models (prediction ≠ intervention; the "shifted distribution" problem; why a model that predicts well can give disastrous policy advice — the example of a model that learns the effect of a treatment given to sicker patients), reinforcement learning as experimentation (one sentence); a closing page of Hill's criteria and the honest statement that observational causal inference is assumption-driven, that the assumptions must be stated as a DAG or equivalent, and that "the data speak for themselves" is false.

Worked examples (≥8): Simpson's paradox with both DAGs and the correct answer for each; the association-vs-ATE decomposition for a toy population of 8 units with both potential outcomes written out (so the selection bias is a number); Fisher's exact test for a 6-unit experiment by full enumeration; standardization and IPW computed by hand on a 2×2×2 table (A, L, Y) and shown equal; the collider: a linear DAG with path coefficients, the covariance via Wright's rules, and the biased regression coefficient when conditioning on the collider (exact number); the backdoor criterion applied to a 5-node DAG with the valid and invalid adjustment sets listed; the Wald IV estimator for a binary instrument from a 2×2 table of means; a 2² factorial design's main effects and interaction from 4 cell means.

Project (numpy/scipy only): a causal simulation library: generate data from a user-specified linear-Gaussian DAG (coefficients on edges), then (a) show the association ≠ ATE under confounding and = ATE under randomization (assert), (b) show standardization and IPW recover the ATE under conditional exchangeability (assert within Monte Carlo error) and fail when a confounder is omitted, (c) the collider trap: conditioning on a collider induces association between independent causes (assert the correlation ≈ 0 before and ≠ 0 after), (d) the mediator trap: adjusting for a mediator removes the effect (assert), (e) M-bias; Wright's path rules verified numerically (sample covariances vs sums over paths); Fisher's randomization test implemented by re-randomization vs the t test on a small experiment; the 2^k factorial design analysis with interaction plots (ME DOE flavor); IV: simulate a confounded treatment with a valid instrument, show OLS biased, 2SLS unbiased (assert), the weak-instrument bias as the first-stage strength shrinks (plot bias vs first-stage F); regression discontinuity on simulated data with a threshold and a local linear fit on each side; difference-in-differences with and without parallel trends (the second showing bias); a sensitivity analysis plot: estimated effect vs assumed strength of an unmeasured confounder; Granger "causality" between two series driven by a common cause (ST-7) showing the predictive relationship exists while the causal one doesn't; the prediction-vs-intervention demo: fit a predictive model on data where treatment was assigned to the sicker, show it predicts well and recommends the wrong policy. "Your turn": a propensity-score matching estimator; a DAG of your own with three adjustment sets, tested by simulation; the Monty Hall problem (F4) drawn as a DAG with the host's choice as a collider.

§8 honest: the potential-outcomes notation looks like overkill until the selection-bias decomposition, and then it's indispensable; "adjust for everything you have" is the most common and most harmful mistake, and colliders are why — simulate it until you believe it; DAGs encode assumptions you cannot test from the data, which is the point, not a weakness; IV's exclusion restriction is almost never testable and almost always arguable; regression coefficients are causal only when a stated DAG says so, and this module should permanently change how you read ST-3's output tables; this is 8 hours on a subject with semester-long courses and a research frontier — it's here because without it every other module in the course is dangerous.
