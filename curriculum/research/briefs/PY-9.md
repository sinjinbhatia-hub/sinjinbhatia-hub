§1 (the problem): real measurements come as tables with labels — a timestamp, a sensor id, a reading, a unit, a flag — with missing cells, duplicated rows, and inconsistent names. NumPy arrays have no labels; a dict of lists has labels but no alignment or grouping. The forcing problem: 50,000 readings from 12 sensors on 3 machines over two weeks, with gaps; you need per-machine daily means, the correlation between two sensors sampled at different rates, and a clean table to regress on (ST-3). Doing that with arrays is index-juggling; with pandas it is a few lines — if you learn to think in aligned, labeled columns. §2 (the intuition): a Series is a NumPy array with an index (labels) glued to it; a DataFrame is a dict of Series sharing one index; every operation aligns on labels first, which is why adding two Series with different indexes gives NaN where only one has data; `groupby` is split–apply–combine; a tidy table has one variable per column and one observation per row, and most pandas pain is an untidy table.

§8 honest: `loc` slices are inclusive on both ends, unlike everything else in Python; the `SettingWithCopyWarning` is pandas telling you it does not know whether you modified a view or a copy — stop using chained indexing instead of silencing it; `groupby` returns a MultiIndex that you will fight until you learn `reset_index` and named aggregation; `merge` silently multiplies rows on duplicate keys — `validate=` is not optional; time zones and daylight saving will corrupt a resample once in your life; `apply(lambda row: ...)` is the slow path and feels natural; most "pandas is confusing" is "my table is not tidy"; pandas changes its API more than NumPy — read deprecation warnings.
