# Brief: QC-6 — Grover search and amplitude amplification
Folder: curriculum/05-quantum-computing/06-grover-amplitude-amplification/ · ~8 hours · prereqs QC-4 (phase oracles, Hadamard transform, query lower bounds), LA-5 (reflections, projections), LA-7 (rotation matrices and eigenvalues) · unlocks QC-9 (amplitude estimation context), algorithm-design intuition
Pair with: Mermin ch. 4; Nielsen & Chuang ch. 6; Preskill ch. 6.4; Grover (1996) "A fast quantum mechanical algorithm for database search"; Brassard, Høyer, Mosca & Tapp (2000) "Quantum amplitude amplification and estimation"; Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard & Vazirani (1997) for the lower bound.
Conventions: search space of N = 2ⁿ items; M marked items (M ≪ N unless stated); the marking oracle O_f|x⟩ = (−1)^{f(x)}|x⟩; the uniform state |s⟩ = H^{⊗n}|0ⁿ⟩; the diffusion operator D = 2|s⟩⟨s| − I; the Grover iterate G = D O_f; the angle θ with sin(θ/2) = √(M/N) (Nielsen & Chuang's convention; state it and warn that Mermin uses half that); the good/bad states |β⟩, |α⟩; amplitude amplification with a general preparation A and a = sin²θ_a.

§1: Grover (1996) found that a quantum computer can search an unstructured list of N items with O(√N) queries instead of N/2; Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard and Vazirani (1997, published the same year) proved no quantum algorithm can do better — the first tight quantum lower bound; Brassard, Høyer, Mosca and Tapp (2000) generalized it to amplitude amplification, which quadratically speeds up any algorithm with a small success probability, and to amplitude estimation, which quadratically speeds up Monte Carlo; the honest assessment: a quadratic speedup is real but fragile — it can be eaten by error-correction overhead (QC-8) and by the cost of the oracle, and for most practical searches it never pays. The forcing problem: QC-4 and QC-5 needed hidden structure; many problems (NP search, constraint satisfaction, database lookup) have no exploitable structure beyond "check a candidate", and the question is whether quantum mechanics helps at all in that setting — the answer is "yes, but only quadratically, and provably no more". §2: the whole algorithm lives in a two-dimensional plane spanned by the uniform superposition of marked states and of unmarked states; the oracle reflects across the unmarked axis, the diffusion reflects across the starting state, and two reflections compose to a rotation (LA-5's reflections; LA-1/LA-7's rotation matrices) by angle θ ≈ 2√(M/N); start near the unmarked axis, rotate about (π/4)√(N/M) times, and you're near the marked axis; over-rotate and you overshoot — the algorithm is a rotation, not a monotone climb.

Topics in order: THE UNSTRUCTURED SEARCH PROBLEM and its classical complexity (N/2 expected queries deterministic; Ω(N) randomized — prove the randomized bound for one marked item); the oracle (QC-4's phase form; implementing f as a reversible circuit; the oracle "knows" the answer only in the sense that it can check it — explain the difference between recognizing and finding, the NP analogy); THE GEOMETRY (the module's central derivation): define |α⟩ = uniform over unmarked, |β⟩ = uniform over marked, show |s⟩ = cos(θ/2)|α⟩ + sin(θ/2)|β⟩ with sin(θ/2) = √(M/N) (derive), show O_f acts on span{|α⟩, |β⟩} as the reflection across |α⟩ (derive), show D = 2|s⟩⟨s| − I is the reflection across |s⟩ (derive — LA-5: I − 2P is a reflection across the complement of P's range; 2P − I across P's range), the composition of two reflections whose axes meet at angle θ/2 is a rotation by θ (prove — LA-1/LA-7: the product of two reflection matrices is a rotation by twice the angle between the mirrors), so G^k|s⟩ = cos((2k+1)θ/2)|α⟩ + sin((2k+1)θ/2)|β⟩ (derive by induction), success probability sin²((2k+1)θ/2), optimal k = ⌊π/(2θ) − ½⌉ ≈ (π/4)√(N/M) (derive), the success probability ≥ 1 − M/N at the optimum (derive the bound from the angle error ≤ θ/2), the overshoot (probability is periodic in k — the "souffle problem"), the eigenvalues of G as e^{±iθ} restricted to the plane (LA-7 — derive; the bridge to amplitude estimation); IMPLEMENTING THE DIFFUSION: D = H^{⊗n}(2|0ⁿ⟩⟨0ⁿ| − I)H^{⊗n} (derive), the conditional phase flip on |0ⁿ⟩ via X's and a multi-controlled Z (Toffoli ladder with ancillas — QC-2's universality constructions; gate count O(n)), "inversion about the mean" interpretation (derive: D maps amplitudes a_x ↦ 2ā − a_x), the full circuit and its cost: O(√N) oracle calls and O(n√N) other gates; UNKNOWN NUMBER OF SOLUTIONS: the problem (k depends on M), the randomized strategy (pick k uniformly from an exponentially growing range — BBHT: state the algorithm and the O(√(N/M)) expected queries; derive the key lemma that for k uniform in [0, K) with K large, the average success probability is ≈ ½), and quantum counting as the alternative (phase estimation on G gives θ, hence M — derive the connection to QC-5); THE LOWER BOUND: any quantum algorithm needs Ω(√N) queries for unstructured search — the BBBV hybrid argument (derive at the level of the key inequality: the total "query magnitude" on any item over T queries is T, so some item receives ≤ T/N of it on average; changing the oracle on that item moves the final state by O(T/√N); distinguishing requires Ω(1) movement, so T = Ω(√N) — do it carefully), or the polynomial-method version (QC-4: OR needs degree Ω(√N) — state Paturi/Nisan–Szegedy), so Grover is optimal and NP-complete problems get at most a quadratic speedup from black-box search (the honest consequence: quantum computers are not believed to solve NP-complete problems efficiently); AMPLITUDE AMPLIFICATION: a general algorithm A (no measurements) with success probability a = sin²(θ_a/2) on "good" states; the operator Q = −A S₀ A^{−1} S_χ (derive that it rotates by θ_a in the good/bad plane, generalizing Grover with A = H^{⊗n}), O(1/√a) repetitions instead of O(1/a) — the quadratic speedup of any randomized algorithm with a check, the "fixed-point" and "oblivious" variants named; AMPLITUDE ESTIMATION: phase estimation (QC-5) on Q estimates θ_a, hence a, with error O(1/T) after T applications vs Monte Carlo's O(1/√T) (derive the error scaling from phase estimation's precision; F5/ST-1: the classical Monte Carlo standard error) — the quadratic speedup for estimating means, the financial-risk/option-pricing application named with the caveat about state-preparation cost; APPLICATIONS AND REALITY: SAT and constraint satisfaction (Grover on a verifier circuit; quadratic speedup over brute force, not over good classical heuristics — be blunt), collision finding (BHT, N^{1/3}) and element distinctness (Ambainis, N^{2/3}) as other query speedups named, the cost model: with error-correction overhead (QC-8) of ~10⁵–10⁹ in time per logical gate vs classical gates, the quadratic speedup only pays for searches far beyond practical sizes — state the Babbush et al. (2021) "focus beyond quadratic speedups" argument with its numbers hedged, quantum walks as the generalization in one paragraph; Grover as a physical process (the continuous-time analogue: H = |s⟩⟨s| + |w⟩⟨w| gives oscillation at frequency ∝ 1/√N — Farhi–Gutmann; derive the two-level Rabi picture — QM-8's Rabi oscillation between |s⟩ and |w⟩; the link to adiabatic search in QC-9).

Worked examples (≥8): N = 4, M = 1: one Grover iteration finds the item with certainty (derive θ = π/3 and (2k+1)θ/2 = π/2 at k = 1); N = 8, M = 1: the success probability after k = 1, 2, 3 iterations (0.781, 0.945, 0.330); the inversion-about-the-mean for amplitudes (½, ½, ½, −½) step by step; the product of two reflections in R² as a rotation by twice the mirror angle (explicit matrices); the optimal k for N = 2²⁰, M = 1 (804) and for M = 16 (201); the BBBV argument's key inequality for N = 10⁶ and the implied query bound; amplitude amplification for a with a = 0.01: classical 100 repetitions vs quantum ~8; amplitude estimation's error with T = 1000 vs Monte Carlo with 1000 samples.

§8 honest: two reflections make a rotation — if the LA-5 reflection formula 2P − I isn't automatic, the module will feel like magic instead of geometry; the success probability oscillates, and stopping at the wrong k is worse than classical — students expect monotone improvement; the √N speedup is optimal (the BBBV argument is short — learn it), so "quantum computers solve NP-complete problems" is false; amplitude estimation's quadratic Monte Carlo speedup is real in query count and usually wiped out by state-preparation and error-correction costs; the 3-SAT comparison exists to make the practical limits visceral; Mermin's and Nielsen & Chuang's θ differ by a factor of 2 — pick one and stay there.
