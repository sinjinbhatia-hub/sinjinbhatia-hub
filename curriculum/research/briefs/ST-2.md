# Brief: ST-2 — Intervals and tests: sampling distributions, pivots, the bootstrap, Neyman–Pearson, likelihood-ratio tests, p-values and their abuse, multiple testing
Folder: curriculum/03-statistical-modeling/02-intervals-and-tests/ · ~12 hours · prereq ST-1 · unlocks ST-3
Pair with: Wasserman ch. 8 (bootstrap), 10 (hypothesis testing); MIT 18.650 ch. 4–5; Rice ch. 9, 11; Efron & Tibshirani *An Introduction to the Bootstrap* ch. 1–6, 12–14; the ASA statement on p-values (2016) and Greenland et al. (2016) "Statistical tests, P values, confidence intervals, and power: a guide to misinterpretations" — required reading; Casella & Berger ch. 8–9 for proofs.
Conventions: confidence level 1 − α; a (1−α) confidence interval C(X) with P_θ(θ ∈ C(X)) ≥ 1 − α for all θ; null H₀, alternative H₁; test statistic T, rejection region R, size α = sup_{H₀} P(reject), power β(θ) = P_θ(reject); p-value; the standard normal quantile z_{α/2}; t_{n−1}, χ²_k, F distributions; bootstrap replicates θ̂*_b, B replicates.

§1: Student (Gosset, 1908) at Guinness needed inference from small samples and found the t distribution; Fisher (1925) made significance testing a tool and p < 0.05 a convention he never meant as a rule; Neyman and Pearson (1933) built the decision-theoretic framework with two error types and power, in open conflict with Fisher; the two frameworks were fused into a textbook hybrid that neither man endorsed; Efron (1979) invented the bootstrap, making inference possible without analytic sampling distributions; the replication crisis (2005–) exposed how badly the hybrid is misused. The forcing problem: a point estimate without a measure of uncertainty is nearly useless, and deciding between hypotheses needs a calibrated procedure — but the calibration (over hypothetical repetitions) is subtle and almost universally misread. §2: a confidence interval is a procedure that captures the truth 95% of the time over repeated experiments — the interval is random, the parameter is fixed; a pivot is a quantity whose distribution doesn't depend on the unknown parameter, which is what makes intervals derivable; a test asks "if the null were true, how surprising is this data?", and the p-value is that surprise — not the probability the null is true; the bootstrap says "the sample is your best estimate of the population, so resample from it to see how your estimator wobbles".

Topics in order: SAMPLING DISTRIBUTIONS for Normal data: X̄ ~ N(μ, σ²/n), (n−1)S²/σ² ~ χ²_{n−1} (prove via the orthogonal decomposition — LA-5: project onto the all-ones direction; the student has the tools, use them), independence of X̄ and S² (prove via the same projection), the t statistic (X̄ − μ)/(S/√n) ~ t_{n−1} (derive from the definition of t as Normal over root-mean-χ²), the F distribution for variance ratios; §8 honest: the interpretation of a confidence interval is the single most misunderstood sentence in science and the module will make you say it correctly ten times; Neyman–Pearson and Fisher are different philosophies and the textbook hybrid is incoherent — know which you're doing; the LRT derivation of the t test is algebra most courses skip, and doing it once makes the "trinity" obvious; the bootstrap is not magic and fails for the max and for small n; p-values are not probabilities of hypotheses and never will be; the multiple-testing section is where real research goes wrong, and the simulations are there to make it visceral; power analysis is routinely skipped in practice and that's why the literature is full of false positives.
