§1: Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio (2014) added attention to a recurrent translator to fix the bottleneck (ML-8's closing derivation); Vaswani et al. (2017) threw the recurrence away and kept only attention — parallel over the sequence, so it could be trained on far more data; Radford et al. (GPT, 2018; GPT-2, 2019) showed that a decoder-only transformer trained to predict the next token learns language and much else; Devlin et al. (BERT, 2018) did the encoder version; Kaplan et al. (2020) found that loss falls as a power law in compute, data, and parameters, and Hoffmann et al. (2022) corrected the exponents — and the scaling-law era followed. The forcing problem: a recurrent network processes a sequence one step at a time (slow to train) through a fixed-size state (lossy), and ML-8's gradient products make long dependencies hard; attention lets every position look directly at every other position in one parallel step, with a path length of 1 between any two tokens. §2: attention is a soft dictionary lookup — each query asks "which keys are like me", the softmax turns the similarities into weights, and the answer is the weighted average of the values; it is ML-1's Nadaraya–Watson kernel smoother with a learned, asymmetric kernel (ML-4), and SM-7's Boltzmann distribution over positions with √d_k as the temperature; multi-head attention runs several lookups in parallel in different subspaces (LA-3/LA-12: the heads are projections); a transformer block is attention (mixing across positions) followed by an MLP (mixing across features), each wrapped in a residual connection (ML-8) and a layer norm (ML-7); positional encodings put the order back, because attention alone is permutation-equivariant (prove it); a language model is a softmax classifier (ML-3) over the next token, trained on text, and the loss in bits per token is F5's cross-entropy with the true distribution.
