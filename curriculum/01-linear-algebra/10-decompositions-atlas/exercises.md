# LA-10 Exercises

Conventions as in the notes: $A^*$ is the conjugate transpose; SPD means symmetric positive definite; the Cholesky factor $L$ is lower triangular with positive diagonal; a flop is one add or multiply, leading term only; point sets for Procrustes are the columns of $X$ (source) and $Y$ (target).

## A. Warm-up (do all; 5–10 minutes each)

**A1.** Compute by hand the Cholesky factor of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 9 & 3 \\ 3 & 5\end{pmatrix}$ using (3.3). Then solve $A\mathbf{x} = (12, 8)^\top$ by one forward and one back substitution, and verify.

**A2.** For $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1\end{pmatrix}$: attempt Cholesky and report where and how it fails. Then write $B = LDL^\top$ with $L$ unit lower triangular, read off from $D$ how many eigenvalues of $B$ are negative, and confirm by computing the eigenvalues.

**A3.** (Flop budget.) You must solve $A\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{b}_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, 50$ with the same dense $1000\times1000$ matrix $A$. Compare (a) calling a solver 50 times (one PLU per call), (b) one PLU and then 50 pairs of triangular solves, (c) as (b) but $A$ is SPD and you use Cholesky. Give flop counts and ratios. What does the ratio (a)/(b) become for 1000 right-hand sides? For what number of right-hand sides do the triangular solves start to cost more than the factorization?

**A4.** $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3\end{pmatrix}$ is already upper triangular, so $Q = I$, $T = A$ is one Schur form. Find a *second* Schur form whose diagonal reads $(3, 2)$: take the unit eigenvector for $3$ as $\mathbf{q}_1$, complete it to $Q$, and compute $T = Q^\top AQ$. Check that $\|T\|_F = \|A\|_F$ and that $|t_{12}|$ is what Corollary 3.8a predicts.

**A5.** Polar decomposition of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 \\ 3 & 0\end{pmatrix}$: compute $A^\top A$, its positive semidefinite square root $P$, and $U = AP^{-1}$. Verify that $U$ is orthogonal and describe it geometrically. Then compute the *left* polar decomposition $A = P'U$ with $P' = (AA^\top)^{1/2}$ and the same $U$, and explain the relation between $P$ and $P'$.

**A6.** For each task, name the factorization from the decision table (§3.9) and give one sentence of justification: (a) the determinant of a dense $400\times400$ matrix; (b) drawing 10,000 samples from $\mathcal N(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)$ with $\Sigma$ a $50\times50$ covariance matrix; (c) the natural frequencies of a 200-degree-of-freedom truss; (d) fitting a plane to $10^5$ noisy points in $\mathbb{R}^3$; (e) deciding whether a $30\times30$ matrix of pairwise measurements has rank 3; (f) the orientation of a drone from 20 matched feature points.

## B. Core (do all)

**B1.** (Cholesky is unique.) Suppose $A = L_1L_1^\top = L_2L_2^\top$ with both $L_i$ lower triangular with positive diagonal. Show that $L_2^{-1}L_1 = L_2^\top L_1^{-\top}$, explain why a matrix that is both lower and upper triangular is diagonal, and conclude $L_1 = L_2$. Where exactly did positivity of the diagonal get used, and what is true without it?

**B2.** (QR and the normal equations.) Let $A$ be $m\times n$ with full column rank and reduced QR factorization $A = QR$. (a) Show $A^\top A = R^\top R$ and explain why, after fixing signs so that $\operatorname{diag}R > 0$, this is *the* Cholesky factorization of $A^\top A$. (b) Show $\kappa_2(A^\top A) = \kappa_2(A)^2$ using the SVD. (c) The least-squares solution via QR is $R\mathbf{x} = Q^\top\mathbf{b}$; via the normal equations it is $R^\top R\mathbf{x} = A^\top\mathbf{b}$. If $\kappa_2(A) = 10^7$ and you compute in double precision (about 16 digits), roughly how many correct digits does each route leave? Use the rule of thumb that a solve with condition number $\kappa$ loses $\log_{10}\kappa$ digits.

**B3.** (Departure from normality.) (a) Prove $\|A\|_F^2 = \sum_i|\lambda_i|^2 + \sum_{i<j}|t_{ij}|^2$ for any Schur form $A = QTQ^*$ (Corollary 3.8a), and deduce that $A$ is normal iff $\|A\|_F^2 = \sum_i|\lambda_i|^2$. (b) Without computing a Schur form, find $\sum_{i<j}|t_{ij}|^2$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ and for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$. (c) Give a $2\times2$ matrix with $\|A\|_F^2 = 100$ both of whose eigenvalues are $0$.

**B4.** Using only "normal $\Rightarrow A = Q\Lambda Q^*$" (Theorem 3.10), prove: (a) a normal matrix with all eigenvalues real is Hermitian; (b) a normal matrix with all eigenvalues on the unit circle is unitary; (c) a normal matrix with all eigenvalues in $\{0, 1\}$ is an orthogonal projection ($A^2 = A = A^*$); (d) for normal $A$, $\|A\mathbf{x}\| = \|A^*\mathbf{x}\|$ for every $\mathbf{x}$.

**B5.** (The $2\times2$ blocks of the real Schur form.) Let $A$ be real with a non-real eigenvalue $\lambda = a + ib$ ($b \ne 0$) and eigenvector $\mathbf{x} + i\mathbf{y}$ with $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}$ real. (a) Separate real and imaginary parts of $A(\mathbf{x} + i\mathbf{y}) = (a + ib)(\mathbf{x} + i\mathbf{y})$ to show $A\mathbf{x} = a\mathbf{x} - b\mathbf{y}$ and $A\mathbf{y} = b\mathbf{x} + a\mathbf{y}$. (b) Conclude that $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}\}$ is invariant under $A$ and that the matrix of $A$ restricted to it, in the basis $(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})$, is $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ -b & a\end{pmatrix}$. (c) Show that $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ are linearly independent. (Hint: if $\mathbf{y} = c\mathbf{x}$ with $c$ real, then $\mathbf{x} + i\mathbf{y} = (1 + ic)\mathbf{x}$ is a complex multiple of a real vector; what does that force about $\lambda$?) (d) Explain in two or three sentences how this replaces steps 2–4 of the proof of Theorem 3.8 to give Theorem 3.11.

**B6.** (Polar decomposition and the nearest orthogonal matrix.) $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5\end{pmatrix}$. (a) Compute $A^\top A$, its eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors, and $P = (A^\top A)^{1/2}$ in closed form. (b) Compute $U = AP^{-1}$ and verify $U^\top U = I$ and $UP = A$. (c) Show that for every orthogonal $Q$, $\|A - U\|_F \le \|A - Q\|_F$, and compute $\|A - U\|_F^2$ exactly as $\sum_i(\sigma_i - 1)^2$. (d) What rotation angle is $U$?

**B7.** (Code.) Add to `project.py` a function `ldlt(A)` that returns the unit lower triangular `L` and the vector of pivots `d` with $A = L\operatorname{diag}(d)L^\top$, by entry matching (adapt (3.3): no square roots). (a) Assert on the Example 4.1 matrix that $d = (4, 4, 4)$ and $L = L_{\text{Chol}}/2$. (b) Run it on $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ and on the modified Example 4.1 matrix with $a_{33} = 1$; compare the number of negative entries of $d$ with the number of negative eigenvalues (Sylvester's law of inertia, notes §3.1; proved in LA-12). (c) In a comment, explain why `cholesky` fails exactly when some $d_j \le 0$ and why `ldlt` can succeed where `cholesky` fails. (d) Find an invertible symmetric matrix on which `ldlt` *without pivoting* fails, and explain what symmetric pivoting (swapping row $i$ and column $i$ together) can and cannot do about it.

**B8.** (Chain of springs.) $n$ unit springs in series with the left end fixed and the right end free have stiffness matrix $K_n$: tridiagonal with $2$ on the diagonal except $(K_n)_{nn} = 1$, and $-1$ on the sub- and superdiagonals. (a) Compute the $LDL^\top$ pivots of $K_3$ by hand and $\det K_3$. (b) Prove by induction that the pivots of $K_n$ are $d_k = (k+1)/k$ for $k < n$ and $d_n = 1/n$, hence $\det K_n = 1$. (c) Free the left end too, so the diagonal becomes $(1, 2, \dots, 2, 1)$. Show that Cholesky fails at the *last* step with radicand exactly $0$, and interpret the null vector mechanically. (d) Why is Cholesky, not pivoted LU, the right factorization for a $10^5$-degree-of-freedom finite-element version of this problem? Mention bandwidth and fill-in.

## C. Challenge (do at least one)

**C1.** (Norms of normal matrices, and transient growth.) (a) Prove that for normal $A$ the singular values are $|\lambda_i|$ and $\|A\|_2 = \max_i|\lambda_i|$. (b) Show that for *every* square $A$, $\|A\|_2 \ge \max_i|\lambda_i|$, and give a $2\times2$ matrix for which $\|A\|_2 > 10^6\max_i|\lambda_i|$. (c) Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 1000 \\ 0 & 0.9\end{pmatrix}$. Compute $A^k$ in closed form, show that $\|A^k\|_2 \to 0$ but grows to several thousand first, and find roughly where the peak is. Explain what this means for the stability of $\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = A\mathbf{x}_k$ and of $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = B\mathbf{x}$ with non-normal $B$ (LA-7, CM-6), and why nothing of the kind can happen for a normal $A$.

**C2.** (Procrustes with scale and translation; extends Theorem 3.14.) Given point sets $X, Y \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times k}$ (columns), find the scalar $s > 0$, orthogonal $R$ and translation $\mathbf{t}$ minimizing $\|sRX + \mathbf{t}\mathbf{1}^\top - Y\|_F^2$, where $\mathbf{1}$ is the all-ones vector in $\mathbb{R}^k$. (a) Show that the optimal $\mathbf{t}$ makes the centroids match, so that you may assume $X$ and $Y$ are centered. (b) For centered data, expand the objective as a quadratic in $s$ and show that for fixed $R$ the minimizer is $s^*(R) = \operatorname{tr}(R^\top M)/\|X\|_F^2$ with $M = YX^\top$. (c) Substitute back and show that the optimal $R$ is still $WV^\top$ from the SVD of $M$, that $s^* = (\sum_i\sigma_i)/\|X\|_F^2$, and that the minimum residual is $\|Y\|_F^2 - (\sum_i\sigma_i)^2/\|X\|_F^2$. (d) Implement this as "Your turn" 3 in `project.py` for the 3D bracket scaled by $1.7$, and recover $s$ to three digits.

**C3.** (Hadamard's inequality from Cholesky.) Let $A$ be SPD with Cholesky factor $L$. (a) Show $a_{jj} = \sum_{k\le j}l_{jk}^2 \ge l_{jj}^2$. (b) Conclude $\det A \le \prod_ja_{jj}$, with equality iff $A$ is diagonal. (c) Deduce that for any real $n\times n$ matrix $B$ with columns $\mathbf{b}_j$, $|\det B| \le \prod_j\|\mathbf{b}_j\|$, by applying (b) to $B^\top B$. Interpret geometrically using LA-6's reading of $|\det B|$ as a volume.

**C4.** (Every matrix is a limit of diagonalizable matrices; what Schur does that Jordan cannot.) Let $A = QTQ^*$ be a Schur form. (a) For $\varepsilon > 0$ define $T_\varepsilon = T + \varepsilon\operatorname{diag}(1, 2, \dots, n)$. Show that for all but finitely many $\varepsilon$ the diagonal entries of $T_\varepsilon$ are distinct, hence $A_\varepsilon = QT_\varepsilon Q^*$ is diagonalizable, and $\|A_\varepsilon - A\|_2 = n\varepsilon$. (b) Use (a) to prove the Cayley–Hamilton theorem $p_A(A) = 0$ ($p_A$ the characteristic polynomial) for all $A$, given that it holds for diagonalizable matrices (which is one line: $p_A(X\Lambda X^{-1}) = Xp_A(\Lambda)X^{-1} = 0$). You will need that the coefficients of $p_A$ are continuous functions of the entries of $A$; say why they are. (c) Explain why the same argument does *not* show that the Jordan form of $A$ is the limit of the Jordan forms of $A_\varepsilon$, and what this says about computing Jordan forms in floating point (LA-11).
